Introduction

This lecture series transitions to the foundational principles of quantum mechanics (QM), a theoretical framework indispensable for understanding the operation and potential of quantum computing. While classical physics adequately describes macroscopic phenomena, the behavior of matter and energy at the atomic and subatomic scales—the realm where quantum computers operate—is governed by the distinct and often counter-intuitive rules of QM. Phenomena such as superposition and entanglement, which are central to quantum computation’s power, have no classical analogue and can only be grasped through the lens of quantum theory.

Our exploration will necessarily be selective, focusing on the core concepts and mathematical formalism required for quantum information science, rather than providing an exhaustive treatment of quantum physics. We will begin with a brief historical overview, touching upon the experimental observations (such as black-body radiation, the photoelectric effect, and atomic spectra) that necessitated the development of quantum theory, moving beyond the limitations of classical mechanics and electromagnetism.

Subsequently, we will introduce the mathematical language of QM, primarily based on linear algebra in complex Hilbert spaces. This framework allows us to rigorously define quantum states (using state vectors or wave functions), physical observables (represented by Hermitian operators), and the processes of measurement and time evolution. The postulates of quantum mechanics will be presented, forming the axiomatic basis for predicting the behavior of quantum systems.

Key concepts flowing from these postulates, including the probabilistic nature of measurement outcomes (Born rule), the collapse of the wave function upon measurement, the deterministic and unitary evolution of isolated quantum systems governed by the Schrödinger equation (realized as quantum gates in computation), and the fundamental limits imposed by the Heisenberg uncertainty principle, will be discussed. We will also touch upon illustrative physical systems, such as the quantum harmonic oscillator and particle spin, which exemplify quantum phenomena and provide physical realizations for qubits—the fundamental units of quantum information. Particular attention will be paid to the formalism relevant to discrete two-level systems (qubits), which are the building blocks of most quantum computers. The objective is to equip you with the essential quantum mechanical background needed to comprehend the principles underlying quantum algorithms and hardware.

Historical Background and Motivation

Quantum mechanics emerged during the late 19th and early 20th centuries as physicists grappled with experimental results that starkly contradicted the predictions of classical physics—the established framework encompassing Newtonian mechanics, Maxwell’s theory of electromagnetism, and classical thermodynamics and statistical mechanics. While extraordinarily successful in describing the macroscopic world, classical physics failed dramatically when applied to phenomena at the atomic and subatomic scales. This section outlines some key experimental challenges that paved the way for the quantum revolution.

Limitations of Classical Physics

Several critical experiments yielded results inexplicable within the classical framework, highlighting the need for a new theoretical foundation.

Black Body Radiation

A black body is an idealized physical body that absorbs all incident electromagnetic radiation, regardless of frequency or angle of incidence. When held at a uniform temperature \(T\), it emits electromagnetic radiation, known as thermal radiation, with a spectrum (intensity distribution across frequencies or wavelengths) that dependsonly on \(T\).

Classical physics, using statistical mechanics (equipartition theorem) and electromagnetism, led to the Rayleigh-Jeans law. This law predicted that the energy density \(\rho(\nu, T)\) of the emitted radiation should be proportional to the square of the frequency: \(\rho(\nu, T) \propto \nu^2 T\). While agreeing with experiments at low frequencies, this prediction diverged catastrophically at high frequencies, implying that a black body should emit an infinite amount of energy, particularly in the ultraviolet range. This theoretical failure became known as the ultraviolet catastrophe.

Experimental measurements, however, showed that the spectrum peaked at a specific frequency (which shifts higher with increasing temperature, Wien’s displacement law) and then decreased rapidly at higher frequencies.

Remark. In 1900, Max Planck resolved this discrepancy by introducing a radical idea: he postulated that the oscillators within the cavity walls could only emit or absorb energy in discrete packets, or "quanta," proportional to the frequency of the radiation. The energy of a single quantum is given by: \[E = h\nu\] where \(\nu\) is the frequency and \(h\) is a new fundamental constant, now known as Planck’s constant (\(h \approx 6.626 \times 10^{-34}\) J\(\cdot\)s). By incorporating this quantization into the statistical treatment, Planck derived a formula that perfectly matched the experimental spectrum (1). This marked the birth of quantum theory, although the full implications were not immediately realized.

Photoelectric Effect

The photoelectric effect involves the emission of electrons (photoelectrons) from the surface of a material (typically a metal) when light is shone upon it. Classical wave theory predicted:

  • The kinetic energy of emitted electrons should increase with the intensity (brightness) of the light, as more intense light carries more energy.

  • Electrons should be emitted for any frequency of light, as long as the intensity is sufficient to accumulate enough energy over time.

  • There might be a noticeable time delay between illumination and electron emission, especially for low-intensity light, as electrons need time to absorb enough energy.

However, experiments revealed a different behavior:

  • The maximum kinetic energy (\(K_{max}\)) of the emitted electrons depends linearly on the frequency (\(\nu\)) of the light, not its intensity.

  • For each material, there exists a characteristic threshold frequency (\(\nu_0\)). No electrons are emitted if the light’s frequency is below this threshold (\(\nu < \nu_0\)), regardless of how intense the light is.

  • Electron emission occurs almost instantaneously upon illumination (\(\nu \ge \nu_0\)), even for very low light intensities.

Remark. In 1905, Albert Einstein provided a revolutionary explanation by extending Planck’s quantum hypothesis. He proposed that light itself is quantized, consisting of discrete particles of energy called photons. Each photon carries an energy \(E = h\nu\). When light interacts with the material, energy is transferred in these discrete photon packets. An electron is ejected only if it absorbs a single photon with sufficient energy to overcome the material’s binding energy, known as the work function (\(W\)). The minimum energy required corresponds to the threshold frequency: \(W = h\nu_0\). If \(h\nu > W\), the excess energy appears as the kinetic energy of the emitted electron: \[K_{max} = h\nu - W = h(\nu - \nu_0) \label{eq:photoelectric}\] This equation perfectly explains the experimental observations (2): \(K_{max}\) depends linearly on \(\nu\), the existence of \(\nu_0\), and the instantaneous emission (as energy transfer occurs via single photon absorption). Increasing light intensity corresponds to increasing the number of photons, leading to more electrons being emitted, but not affecting the energy of individual photons or the \(K_{max}\) of electrons. This work, which firmly established the particle nature of light, earned Einstein the 1921 Nobel Prize in Physics.

Atomic Spectra (Hydrogen Atom)

When gases at low pressure are subjected to an electric discharge or high temperature, they emit light. Unlike the continuous spectrum emitted by a hot solid (like a black body), the light emitted by excited gases consists of a series of discrete, sharp lines at specific frequencies (or wavelengths). Each element has a unique characteristic line spectrum. Conversely, when continuous white light passes through a cool gas, the gas absorbs light at precisely the same frequencies that it emits when excited, resulting in dark lines in the continuous spectrum (absorption spectrum).

Classical physics, based on the model of electrons orbiting the nucleus, could not explain these discrete spectra. According to classical electromagnetism, an accelerating electron (like one in orbit) should continuously radiate energy, causing it to spiral into the nucleus, and producing a continuous spectrum of radiation—neither of which is observed. Atoms are stable, and their spectra are discrete. The spectrum of hydrogen, the simplest atom, showed remarkable regularity (e.g., the Balmer series in the visible region), described empirically by the Rydberg formula, but lacked a theoretical explanation.

Early Quantum Ideas: Bohr’s Model

In 1913, Niels Bohr proposed a model for the hydrogen atom that, while semi-classical, successfully explained its discrete spectrum by incorporating Planck’s quantum ideas.

Quantization of Angular Momentum

Bohr postulated that the electron could only exist in certain stable, non-radiating circular orbits around the nucleus. These allowed orbits were characterized by a condition quantizing the electron’s orbital angular momentum (\(L\)). He proposed that the angular momentum must be an integer multiple of \(\hbar= h / 2\pi\) (read "h-bar"): \[L = m_e v r = n \hbar, \quad n = 1, 2, 3, \dots \label{eq:bohr_quantization}\] where \(m_e\) is the electron mass, \(v\) is its orbital speed, \(r\) is the orbit’s radius, and \(n\) is an integer called the principal quantum number.

Derivation of Quantized Energy Levels

Bohr combined this quantum condition with classical mechanics. For an electron (charge \(-e\)) orbiting a proton (charge \(+e\)), the electrostatic Coulomb force provides the necessary centripetal force for a circular orbit: \[\frac{k_e e^2}{r^2} = \frac{m_e v^2}{r} \label{eq:coulomb_centripetal}\] where \(k_e = 1 / (4\pi\epsilon_0)\) is Coulomb’s constant in SI units. The total energy \(E\) is the sum of kinetic energy \(K = \frac{1}{2} m_e v^2\) and potential energy \(V = -k_e e^2 / r\): \[E = K + V = \frac{1}{2} m_e v^2 - \frac{k_e e^2}{r} \label{eq:classical_energy}\] From [eq:coulomb_centripetal], we have \(m_e v^2 = k_e e^2 / r\), which implies \(K = \frac{1}{2} (k_e e^2 / r) = -V/2\). Therefore, the total energy can be expressed solely in terms of the radius: \(E = K + V = -V/2 + V = V/2 = -K\), specifically: \[E = -\frac{1}{2} \frac{k_e e^2}{r}\] By simultaneously solving the force balance equation [eq:coulomb_centripetal] and the angular momentum quantization condition [eq:bohr_quantization], Bohr derived expressions for the allowed radii \(r_n\) and the corresponding allowed energy levels \(E_n\): \[\begin{aligned} r_n &= \frac{n^2 \hbar^2}{m_e k_e e^2} = n^2 a_0 \\ E_n &= -\frac{1}{2} \frac{k_e e^2}{r_n} = -\left(\frac{m_e k_e^2 e^4}{2 \hbar^2}\right) \frac{1}{n^2} = -\frac{E_R}{n^2} \label{eq:bohr_energy}\end{aligned}\] Here, \(a_0 = \hbar^2 / (m_e k_e e^2) \approx 0.0529\) nm is the Bohr radius (radius of the \(n=1\) orbit), and \(E_R = m_e k_e^2 e^4 / (2 \hbar^2) \approx 13.6\) eV is the Rydberg energy. The model predicted that the electron could only occupy orbits with these specific radii and energies; all other energies were forbidden.

Explanation of the Hydrogen Spectrum

Bohr further postulated that an electron could make a transition, or "quantum jump," from a higher energy orbit \(E_i\) (with quantum number \(n_i\)) to a lower energy orbit \(E_f\) (with quantum number \(n_f\)) by emitting a single photon. The energy of the emitted photon must equal the energy difference between the initial and final states, conserving energy: \[E_{\text{photon}} = h\nu = \frac{hc}{\lambda} = E_i - E_f\] Substituting the allowed energy levels from [eq:bohr_energy]: \[h\nu = \left(-\frac{E_R}{n_i^2}\right) - \left(-\frac{E_R}{n_f^2}\right) = E_R \left( \frac{1}{n_f^2} - \frac{1}{n_i^2} \right)\] This directly leads to a prediction for the frequency \(\nu\) or the inverse wavelength \(1/\lambda\) (wavenumber) of the emitted spectral lines: \[\frac{1}{\lambda} = \frac{E_R}{hc} \left( \frac{1}{n_f^2} - \frac{1}{n_i^2} \right) = R_H \left( \frac{1}{n_f^2} - \frac{1}{n_i^2} \right) \label{eq:rydberg_formula}\] where \(R_H = E_R / (hc)\) is the Rydberg constant. This formula, derived from Bohr’s postulates, precisely matched the empirical Rydberg formula and accurately predicted the wavelengths of all observed spectral lines of hydrogen (e.g., the Lyman series with \(n_f=1\), Balmer series with \(n_f=2\), Paschen series with \(n_f=3\), etc.) by choosing appropriate integer values for \(n_i\) and \(n_f\) (\(n_i > n_f\)).

Remark. Bohr’s model was a monumental achievement, providing the first quantitative explanation for atomic spectra and introducing the concept of quantized energy levels and quantum jumps (3). However, it was fundamentally flawed:

  • It worked well only for hydrogen and hydrogen-like ions (single-electron systems).

  • It could not explain the relative intensities of spectral lines.

  • It failed to account for the splitting of spectral lines in magnetic fields (Zeeman effect).

  • It mixed classical and quantum concepts in an ad hoc manner, lacking a consistent theoretical foundation (e.g., why were only certain orbits stable?).

Despite its limitations, Bohr’s model was a crucial stepping stone, highlighting the necessity of quantization and paving the way for the development of a complete theory of quantum mechanics over the next decade.

Development of Modern Quantum Mechanics

The limitations of the Bohr model and the growing body of experimental evidence spurred efforts to develop a more comprehensive and consistent quantum theory. This culminated in the mid-1920s with the emergence of two mathematically distinct but physically equivalent formulations.

Heisenberg’s Matrix Mechanics (1925)

Werner Heisenberg, collaborating with Max Born and Pascual Jordan, formulated matrix mechanics. In this abstract approach, classical physical quantities like position (\(x\)) and momentum (\(p\)) were replaced by infinite-dimensional matrices (\(\hat{X}, \hat{P}\)). The dynamics of the system were described by matrix equations analogous to classical equations of motion. A key feature was the inherent non-commutativity of these matrices, reflecting the physical reality that the order of measurements can matter. Specifically, Heisenberg postulated the canonical commutation relation: \[[\hat{X}, \hat{P}] = \hat{X}\hat{P} - \hat{P}\hat{X} = i\hbar\hat{I}\] where \(\hat{I}\) is the identity matrix. Matrix mechanics provided a rigorous framework for calculating quantized energy levels and transition probabilities, successfully generalizing Bohr’s results and other quantization rules. However, its abstract mathematical nature made it difficult for many physicists to grasp intuitively.

Schrödinger’s Wave Mechanics (1926)

Inspired by Louis de Broglie’s hypothesis (1924) that particles like electrons also exhibit wave-like properties (with wavelength \(\lambda = h/p\)), Erwin Schrödinger developed wave mechanics. In this formulation, the state of a quantum system (e.g., an electron) is described by a complex-valued wave function, \(\psi(x, t)\). This wave function contains all possible information about the system and its evolution in time is governed by the Schrödinger equation, a partial differential equation. For a non-relativistic particle of mass \(m\) moving in a potential \(V(x, t)\), the equation is: \[i\hbar\frac{\partial}{\partial t} \psi(x, t) = \left[ -\frac{\hbar^2}{2m} \frac{\partial^2}{\partial x^2} + V(x, t) \right] \psi(x, t) = \hat{H} \psi(x, t) \label{eq:schrodinger_general_1d}\] where \(\hat{H}\) is the Hamiltonian operator, representing the total energy of the system. Schrödinger showed that solving this equation for the hydrogen atom yielded the same quantized energy levels as Bohr’s model, but from a more fundamental basis. The wave mechanics approach, drawing analogies with classical waves, was often found more intuitive and easier to work with mathematically for many problems.

Equivalence and the Copenhagen Interpretation

Schrödinger himself, along with others like Carl Eckart and Paul Dirac, soon demonstrated that matrix mechanics and wave mechanics, despite their different mathematical appearances, were entirely equivalent. They represented different mathematical bases for the same underlying physical theory. Dirac later developed a more abstract and powerful formalism using bras and kets, unifying both approaches within the framework of linear algebra on Hilbert spaces.

The physical interpretation of the wave function \(\psi\) remained a subject of debate until Max Born (1926) proposed that the square of its magnitude, \(|\psi(x, t)|^2\), represents the probability density of finding the particle at position \(x\) at time \(t\). This probabilistic interpretation became a cornerstone of the standard, or Copenhagen interpretation, of quantum mechanics, developed primarily by Bohr, Heisenberg, Born, Pauli, and Dirac. Key tenets of this interpretation include:

  • Wave-particle duality: Quantum objects exhibit both wave-like and particle-like properties depending on the experimental context.

  • Probabilistic outcomes: Measurements generally yield probabilistic results, predictable only in terms of probabilities given by the wave function (Born rule).

  • Uncertainty principle: There are fundamental limits to the precision with which certain pairs of complementary variables (like position and momentum) can be simultaneously known.

  • Measurement disturbance: The act of measurement inevitably disturbs the quantum system, causing its wave function to "collapse" into a state corresponding to the measured outcome.

  • Correspondence principle: In the limit of large quantum numbers or macroscopic systems, quantum mechanics should reproduce the results of classical physics.

The development of matrix mechanics and wave mechanics, their unification, and the establishment of the Copenhagen interpretation during 1925-1927 marked the completion of the foundations of modern non-relativistic quantum mechanics, the theory that underpins quantum computing.

Mathematical Formalism and Postulates

Modern quantum mechanics is formulated using the language of linear algebra over complex numbers, specifically within the framework of Hilbert spaces. This mathematical structure provides the foundation for describing quantum states, observables, measurements, and dynamics.

Hilbert Spaces and State Vectors

The state space of a quantum system is postulated to be a Hilbert space, which is a complex vector space equipped with an inner product, and which is complete with respect to the norm induced by the inner product.

State Vectors (Kets)

An instantaneous state of a quantum system is represented by a state vector, typically denoted using Dirac’s ket notation as \(|\psi\rangle\). This vector belongs to the system’s Hilbert space \(\mathcal{H}\).

  • For systems with a finite number of discrete states (like a qubit), the Hilbert space is finite-dimensional. For a single qubit, the space is \(\mathcal{H} = \mathbb{C}^2\), spanned by the basis vectors \(|0\rangle\) and \(|1\rangle\). A general state is \(|\psi\rangle = \alpha|0\rangle + \beta|1\rangle\), where \(\alpha, \beta \in \mathbb{C}\).

  • For systems with continuous degrees of freedom (like the position of a particle in 1D), the Hilbert space is infinite-dimensional. A common example is the space of square-integrable complex functions, \(L^2(\mathbb{R})\), where states are represented by wave functions \(\psi(x)\).

Inner Product (Bra-Ket Notation)

The Hilbert space is equipped with an inner product, denoted \(\langle\phi|\psi\rangle\), which takes two state vectors \(|\phi\rangle\) and \(|\psi\rangle\) and returns a complex number. It satisfies:

  1. Linearity in the second argument (ket): \(\langle\phi|\alpha\psi_1 + \beta\psi_2\rangle = \alpha\langle\phi|\psi_1\rangle + \beta\langle\phi|\psi_2\rangle\).

  2. Conjugate symmetry: \(\langle\phi|\psi\rangle = (\langle\psi|\phi\rangle)^*\). (This implies anti-linearity in the first argument: \(\langle\alpha\phi_1 + \beta\phi_2|\psi\rangle = \alpha^*\langle\phi_1|\psi\rangle + \beta^*\langle\phi_2|\psi\rangle\)).

  3. Positive-definiteness: \(\langle\psi|\psi\rangle \ge 0\), with equality if and only if \(|\psi\rangle = \mathbf{0}\) (the zero vector).

The inner product allows us to define orthogonality (\(\langle\phi|\psi\rangle = 0\)) and the norm (or length) of a state vector: \(\|| \psi \rangle \| = \sqrt{\langle\psi|\psi\rangle}\).

Dirac notation introduces "bra" vectors \(\langle\phi|\), which are elements of the dual space \(\mathcal{H}^*\) (the space of linear functionals mapping kets to complex numbers). There is a one-to-one correspondence between kets \(|\phi\rangle\) and bras \(\langle\phi|\), such that applying the bra \(\langle\phi|\) to the ket \(|\psi\rangle\) yields the inner product \(\langle\phi|\psi\rangle\). In matrix representation for finite dimensions, if \(|\psi\rangle\) is a column vector, \(\langle\psi|\) is the correspondingconjugate transpose row vector (\(\langle\psi| = |\psi\rangle^\dagger\)).

  • For \(|\psi\rangle, |\phi\rangle \in \mathbb{C}^d\) (column vectors): \(\langle\phi|\psi\rangle = |\phi\rangle^\dagger |\psi\rangle = \sum_{i=1}^d \phi_i^* \psi_i\).

  • For wave functions \(\psi(x), \phi(x) \in L^2(\mathbb{R})\): \(\langle\phi|\psi\rangle = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \phi^*(x) \psi(x) dx\).

Orthonormal Bases and Completeness

A set of vectors \(\{|u_i\rangle\}\) forms an orthonormal basis for \(\mathcal{H}\) if they are mutually orthogonal and normalized to unit length: \[\langle u_i | u_j \rangle = \delta_{ij} \quad (\text{Kronecker delta})\] and if any vector \(|\psi\rangle \in \mathcal{H}\) can be expressed as a linear combination of these basis vectors: \[|\psi\rangle = \sum_i c_i |u_i\rangle\] The coefficients \(c_i\) are complex numbers given by the projection of \(|\psi\rangle\) onto the basis vector \(|u_i\rangle\): \[c_i = \langle u_i | \psi \rangle\] Substituting this back gives \(|\psi\rangle = \sum_i \langle u_i | \psi \rangle |u_i\rangle = \sum_i |u_i\rangle \langle u_i | \psi \rangle\). Since this holds for any \(|\psi\rangle\), we arrive at the completeness relation or resolution of the identity: \[\sum_i |u_i\rangle \langle u_i| = \hat{I} \label{eq:completeness}\] where \(\hat{I}\) is the identity operator on \(\mathcal{H}\). The term \(|u_i\rangle \langle u_i|\) is an outer product, representing a projection operator onto the state \(|u_i\rangle\).

Postulates of Quantum Mechanics

The behavior of quantum systems is governed by a set of fundamental postulates.

Associated with any isolated physical system is a complex vector space with an inner product (i.e., a Hilbert space \(\mathcal{H}\)) known as the state space of the system. The system is completely described by its state vector, which is a unit vector in its state space.

Remark. The requirement that the state vector \(|\psi\rangle\) be a unit vector means \(\langle\psi|\psi\rangle = 1\). This reflects the probabilistic interpretation (total probability must be 1). Furthermore, state vectors differing only by a global phase factor \(e^{i\theta}\) (where \(\theta \in \mathbb{R}\)) represent the same physical state, i.e., \(|\psi\rangle\) and \(e^{i\theta}|\psi\rangle\) are physically indistinguishable. This is because all physically measurable quantities (probabilities, expectation values) depend on terms like \(|\langle\phi|\psi\rangle|^2\) or \(\langle\psi|\hat{A}|\psi\rangle\), which are unaffected by such a phase factor.

The state space for a single qubit is the two-dimensional complex Hilbert space \(\mathcal{H} = \mathbb{C}^2\). A standard orthonormal basis is the computational basis \(\{|0\rangle, |1\rangle\}\), where \[|0\rangle = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}, \quad |1\rangle = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}\] A general state vector is \(|\psi\rangle = \alpha|0\rangle + \beta|1\rangle = \begin{pmatrix} \alpha \\ \beta \end{pmatrix}\), where \(\alpha, \beta \in \mathbb{C}\). The normalization condition requires \(|\alpha|^2 + |\beta|^2 = 1\). States like \(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(|0\rangle + |1\rangle)\) or \(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(|0\rangle - i|1\rangle)\) are valid qubit states representing superpositions.

With every physically measurable quantity \(A\) (an observable), there is associated a Hermitian operator \(\hat{A}\) acting on the state space \(\mathcal{H}\). The possible outcomes of a measurement of \(A\) are the eigenvalues of \(\hat{A}\).

An operator \(\hat{A}\) is Hermitian (or self-adjoint) if it is equal to its adjoint (conjugate transpose): \(\hat{A} = \hat{A}^\dagger\). Key properties of Hermitian operators:

  • Their eigenvalues are always real numbers.

  • Their eigenvectors corresponding to distinct eigenvalues are orthogonal.

  • Their eigenvectors can always form an orthonormal basis for the Hilbert space \(\mathcal{H}\) (Spectral Theorem).

If \(\hat{A}|a_n\rangle = a_n |a_n\rangle\), then \(a_n\) are the real eigenvalues (possible measurement outcomes) and \(|a_n\rangle\) are the corresponding eigenvectors (states associated with those outcomes). The set \(\{|a_n\rangle\}\) forms an orthonormal basis satisfying \(\langle a_m | a_n \rangle = \delta_{mn}\) and \(\sum_n |a_n\rangle \langle a_n| = \hat{I}\).

Important observables for a single qubit are represented by the Pauli operators: \[\begin{aligned} \sigma_x = \hat{X} &= \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix} \quad (\text{Bit flip}) \\ \sigma_y = \hat{Y} &= \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -i \\ i & 0 \end{pmatrix} \\ \sigma_z = \hat{Z} &= \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & -1 \end{pmatrix} \quad (\text{Phase flip})\end{aligned}\] These are all Hermitian matrices. Their eigenvalues are \(+1\) and \(-1\).

  • For \(\hat{Z}\): Eigenvalues are \(+1, -1\). Eigenvectors are \(|0\rangle\) (for \(+1\)) and \(|1\rangle\) (for \(-1\)). Measuring \(\hat{Z}\) corresponds to measuring the qubit in the computational basis.

  • For \(\hat{X}\): Eigenvalues are \(+1, -1\). Eigenvectors are \(|+\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(|0\rangle + |1\rangle)\) (for \(+1\)) and \(|-\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(|0\rangle - |1\rangle)\) (for \(-1\)). Measuring \(\hat{X}\) corresponds to measuring in the Hadamard basis \(\{|+\rangle, |-\rangle\}\).

If a measurement of the observable \(A\) (corresponding to operator \(\hat{A}\)) is performed on a system in the normalized state \(|\psi\rangle\), the probability of obtaining the eigenvalue \(a_n\) (assuming it is non-degenerate for simplicity) is given by: \[P(a_n) = |\langle a_n | \psi \rangle|^2 \label{eq:born_rule}\] where \(|a_n\rangle\) is the normalized eigenvector of \(\hat{A}\) corresponding to the eigenvalue \(a_n\). If the measurement yields the result \(a_n\), the state of the system immediately after the measurement collapses to the corresponding eigenvector \(|a_n\rangle\).

If the eigenvalue \(a_n\) is degenerate, let \(\hat{P}_n = \sum_{i} |a_{n,i}\rangle \langle a_{n,i}|\) be the projection operator onto the eigenspace corresponding to \(a_n\) (where \(\{|a_{n,i}\rangle\}\) is an orthonormal basis for that eigenspace). Then the probability of measuring \(a_n\) is: \[P(a_n) = \langle \psi | \hat{P}_n | \psi \rangle = \sum_i |\langle a_{n,i} | \psi \rangle|^2 \label{eq:born_rule_degenerate}\] And the state after measurement collapses to: \[|\psi'\rangle = \frac{\hat{P}_n |\psi\rangle}{\sqrt{\langle \psi | \hat{P}_n | \psi \rangle}} \label{eq:collapse_degenerate}\]

Remark. The average value, or expectation value, obtained from many measurements of \(A\) on identically prepared systems in state \(|\psi\rangle\) is: \[\langle \hat{A} \rangle = \sum_n a_n P(a_n) = \sum_n a_n |\langle a_n | \psi \rangle|^2\] Using the spectral decomposition \(\hat{A} = \sum_n a_n |a_n\rangle \langle a_n|\) and the completeness relation, this can be written more compactly as: \[\langle \hat{A} \rangle = \langle \psi | \hat{A} | \psi \rangle \label{eq:expectation_value}\]

The evolution of a closed quantum system is described by the Schrödinger equation: \[i\hbar\frac{d}{dt} |\psi(t)\rangle = \hat{H} |\psi(t)\rangle \label{eq:schrodinger_abstract}\] where \(\hbar= h/2\pi\) is the reduced Planck constant, and \(\hat{H}\) is the Hamiltonian operator of the system, which corresponds to the total energy observable.

If the Hamiltonian \(\hat{H}\) is time-independent, the solution to the Schrödinger equation is: \[|\psi(t)\rangle = \hat{U}(t, t_0) |\psi(t_0)\rangle = e^{-i\hat{H}(t-t_0)/\hbar} |\psi(t_0)\rangle \label{eq:time_evolution_operator}\] where \(\hat{U}(t, t_0) = e^{-i\hat{H}(t-t_0)/\hbar}\) is the time evolution operator. This operator is unitary, meaning \(\hat{U}^\dagger \hat{U} = \hat{U} \hat{U}^\dagger = \hat{I}\). Unitary evolution preserves the inner product between states and, consequently, the normalization of state vectors. Quantum computation is primarily concerned with implementing specific sequences of unitary transformations (quantum gates) on qubit states.

Remark. Quantum mechanics describes two distinct types of state evolution:

  1. Unitary evolution: Deterministic, continuous, and reversible evolution of an isolated system according to the Schrödinger equation ([post:time_evolution]). This is how quantum states evolve between measurements or interactions with the environment. Quantum gates perform unitary evolution.

  2. Measurement: Probabilistic, discontinuous, and irreversible change in the state upon interaction with a measurement apparatus ([post:measurement]). This "collapse" projects the state onto one of the eigenstates of the measured observable.

The interplay between these two processes is fundamental to quantum mechanics and quantum computation.

The state space of a composite physical system is the tensor product of the state spaces of the individual component systems. If system 1 is described by Hilbert space \(\mathcal{H}_1\) and system 2 by \(\mathcal{H}_2\), the composite system is described by the Hilbert space \(\mathcal{H} = \mathcal{H}_1 \otimes \mathcal{H}_2\). If system 1 is in state \(|\psi_1\rangle \in \mathcal{H}_1\) and system 2 is in state \(|\psi_2\rangle \in \mathcal{H}_2\), the state of the composite system is \(|\psi_1\rangle \otimes |\psi_2\rangle\), often written as \(|\psi_1\rangle |\psi_2\rangle\) or \(|\psi_1, \psi_2\rangle\).

Remark. The dimension of the composite Hilbert space is the product of the dimensions of the component spaces: \(\dim(\mathcal{H}_1 \otimes \mathcal{H}_2) = \dim(\mathcal{H}_1) \times \dim(\mathcal{H}_2)\). A crucial consequence of the tensor product structure is the existence of entangled states. These are states of a composite system that cannot be written as a simple product state \(|\psi_1\rangle \otimes |\psi_2\rangle\). For example, the Bell state \(|\Phi^+\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(|00\rangle + |11\rangle)\) in \(\mathbb{C}^2 \otimes \mathbb{C}^2\) is entangled. Entanglement represents correlations between quantum systems that are stronger than any possible classical correlation and is a key resource in quantum computation and communication.

The state space for two qubits is \(\mathcal{H} = \mathbb{C}^2 \otimes \mathbb{C}^2 = \mathbb{C}^4\). A basis for this space is formed by the tensor products of the single-qubit basis states: \[\begin{aligned} |00\rangle &= |0\rangle \otimes |0\rangle = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix} \otimes \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix} \\ |01\rangle &= |0\rangle \otimes |1\rangle = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix} \otimes \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix} \\ |10\rangle &= |1\rangle \otimes |0\rangle = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix} \otimes \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix} \\ |11\rangle &= |1\rangle \otimes |1\rangle = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix} \otimes \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ 0 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}\end{aligned}\] A general two-qubit state is \(|\psi\rangle = c_{00}|00\rangle + c_{01}|01\rangle + c_{10}|10\rangle + c_{11}|11\rangle\), with \(\sum_{ij} |c_{ij}|^2 = 1\). The state \(\frac{1}{2}(|00\rangle + |01\rangle + |10\rangle + |11\rangle)\) is a product state: \(\left(\frac{|0\rangle+|1\rangle}{\sqrt{2}}\right) \otimes \left(\frac{|0\rangle+|1\rangle}{\sqrt{2}}\right) = |+\rangle \otimes |+\rangle\). The state \(|\Phi^+\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(|00\rangle + |11\rangle)\) is entangled.

These postulates form the bedrock upon which the theory of quantum mechanics is built, providing the rules for describing quantum states, predicting measurement outcomes, and understanding how quantum systems evolve and combine. They are the essential tools needed to analyze and design quantum algorithms and understand the workings of quantum computers.

# Introduction {#sec:introduction}

This lecture series transitions to the foundational principles of
quantum mechanics (QM), a theoretical framework indispensable for
understanding the operation and potential of quantum computing. While
classical physics adequately describes macroscopic phenomena, the
behavior of matter and energy at the atomic and subatomic scales---the
realm where quantum computers operate---is governed by the distinct and
often counter-intuitive rules of QM. Phenomena such as superposition and
entanglement, which are central to quantum computation's power, have no
classical analogue and can only be grasped through the lens of quantum
theory.

Our exploration will necessarily be selective, focusing on the core
concepts and mathematical formalism required for quantum information
science, rather than providing an exhaustive treatment of quantum
physics. We will begin with a brief historical overview, touching upon
the experimental observations (such as black-body radiation, the
photoelectric effect, and atomic spectra) that necessitated the
development of quantum theory, moving beyond the limitations of
classical mechanics and electromagnetism.

Subsequently, we will introduce the mathematical language of QM,
primarily based on linear algebra in complex Hilbert spaces. This
framework allows us to rigorously define quantum states (using state
vectors or wave functions), physical observables (represented by
Hermitian operators), and the processes of measurement and time
evolution. The postulates of quantum mechanics will be presented,
forming the axiomatic basis for predicting the behavior of quantum
systems.

Key concepts flowing from these postulates, including the probabilistic
nature of measurement outcomes (Born rule), the collapse of the wave
function upon measurement, the deterministic and unitary evolution of
isolated quantum systems governed by the Schrödinger equation (realized
as quantum gates in computation), and the fundamental limits imposed by
the Heisenberg uncertainty principle, will be discussed. We will also
touch upon illustrative physical systems, such as the quantum harmonic
oscillator and particle spin, which exemplify quantum phenomena and
provide physical realizations for qubits---the fundamental units of
quantum information. Particular attention will be paid to the formalism
relevant to discrete two-level systems (qubits), which are the building
blocks of most quantum computers. The objective is to equip you with the
essential quantum mechanical background needed to comprehend the
principles underlying quantum algorithms and hardware.

# Historical Background and Motivation {#sec:historical_background}

Quantum mechanics emerged during the late 19th and early 20th centuries
as physicists grappled with experimental results that starkly
contradicted the predictions of classical physics---the established
framework encompassing Newtonian mechanics, Maxwell's theory of
electromagnetism, and classical thermodynamics and statistical
mechanics. While extraordinarily successful in describing the
macroscopic world, classical physics failed dramatically when applied to
phenomena at the atomic and subatomic scales. This section outlines some
key experimental challenges that paved the way for the quantum
revolution.

## Limitations of Classical Physics {#sec:limitations_classical_physics}

Several critical experiments yielded results inexplicable within the
classical framework, highlighting the need for a new theoretical
foundation.

### Black Body Radiation {#sssec:black_body}

A black body is an idealized physical body that absorbs all incident
electromagnetic radiation, regardless of frequency or angle of
incidence. When held at a uniform temperature $T$, it emits
electromagnetic radiation, known as thermal radiation, with a spectrum
(intensity distribution across frequencies or wavelengths) that
dependsonly on $T$.

Classical physics, using statistical mechanics (equipartition theorem)
and electromagnetism, led to the Rayleigh-Jeans law. This law predicted
that the energy density $\rho(\nu, T)$ of the emitted radiation should
be proportional to the square of the frequency:
$\rho(\nu, T) \propto \nu^2 T$. While agreeing with experiments at low
frequencies, this prediction diverged catastrophically at high
frequencies, implying that a black body should emit an infinite amount
of energy, particularly in the ultraviolet range. This theoretical
failure became known as the **ultraviolet catastrophe**.

Experimental measurements, however, showed that the spectrum peaked at a
specific frequency (which shifts higher with increasing temperature,
Wien's displacement law) and then decreased rapidly at higher
frequencies.

<figcaption>Comparison of black body radiation spectra: Experimental
results (following Planck’s law) versus the classical Rayleigh-Jeans
prediction, highlighting the ultraviolet catastrophe.</figcaption>

::: remark
In 1900, Max Planck resolved this discrepancy by introducing a radical
idea: he postulated that the oscillators within the cavity walls could
only emit or absorb energy in discrete packets, or \"quanta,\"
proportional to the frequency of the radiation. The energy of a single
quantum is given by: $$E = h\nu$$ where $\nu$ is the frequency and $h$
is a new fundamental constant, now known as **Planck's constant**
($h \approx 6.626 \times 10^{-34}$ J$\cdot$s). By incorporating this
quantization into the statistical treatment, Planck derived a formula
that perfectly matched the experimental spectrum
([1](#fig:black_body){reference-type="ref+Label"
reference="fig:black_body"}). This marked the birth of quantum theory,
although the full implications were not immediately realized.
:::

### Photoelectric Effect {#sssec:photoelectric}

The photoelectric effect involves the emission of electrons
(photoelectrons) from the surface of a material (typically a metal) when
light is shone upon it. Classical wave theory predicted:

-   The kinetic energy of emitted electrons should increase with the
    intensity (brightness) of the light, as more intense light carries
    more energy.

-   Electrons should be emitted for any frequency of light, as long as
    the intensity is sufficient to accumulate enough energy over time.

-   There might be a noticeable time delay between illumination and
    electron emission, especially for low-intensity light, as electrons
    need time to absorb enough energy.

However, experiments revealed a different behavior:

-   The maximum kinetic energy ($K_{max}$) of the emitted electrons
    depends linearly on the frequency ($\nu$) of the light, not its
    intensity.

-   For each material, there exists a characteristic **threshold
    frequency** ($\nu_0$). No electrons are emitted if the light's
    frequency is below this threshold ($\nu < \nu_0$), regardless of how
    intense the light is.

-   Electron emission occurs almost instantaneously upon illumination
    ($\nu \ge \nu_0$), even for very low light intensities.

<figcaption>Schematic representation of the photoelectric effect
results: Maximum kinetic energy of photoelectrons versus the frequency
of incident light. Below the threshold frequency <span
class="math inline">\(\nu_0\)</span>, no electrons are emitted. Above
<span class="math inline">\(\nu_0\)</span>, <span
class="math inline">\(K_{max}\)</span> increases linearly with <span
class="math inline">\(\nu\)</span>. The slope of the line is Planck’s
constant <span class="math inline">\(h\)</span>, and the negative

class="math inline">\(W = h\nu_0\)</span>.</figcaption>
</figure>

::: remark
In 1905, Albert Einstein provided a revolutionary explanation by
extending Planck's quantum hypothesis. He proposed that light itself is
quantized, consisting of discrete particles of energy called
**photons**. Each photon carries an energy $E = h\nu$. When light
interacts with the material, energy is transferred in these discrete
photon packets. An electron is ejected only if it absorbs a single
photon with sufficient energy to overcome the material's binding energy,
known as the work function ($W$). The minimum energy required
corresponds to the threshold frequency: $W = h\nu_0$. If $h\nu > W$, the
excess energy appears as the kinetic energy of the emitted electron:
$$K_{max} = h\nu - W = h(\nu - \nu_0)    \label{eq:photoelectric}$$ This
equation perfectly explains the experimental observations
([2](#fig:photoelectric){reference-type="ref+Label"
reference="fig:photoelectric"}): $K_{max}$ depends linearly on $\nu$,
the existence of $\nu_0$, and the instantaneous emission (as energy
transfer occurs via single photon absorption). Increasing light
intensity corresponds to increasing the number of photons, leading to
more electrons being emitted, but not affecting the energy of individual
photons or the $K_{max}$ of electrons. This work, which firmly
established the particle nature of light, earned Einstein the 1921 Nobel
Prize in Physics.
:::

### Atomic Spectra (Hydrogen Atom) {#sssec:atomic_spectra}

When gases at low pressure are subjected to an electric discharge or
high temperature, they emit light. Unlike the continuous spectrum
emitted by a hot solid (like a black body), the light emitted by excited
gases consists of a series of discrete, sharp lines at specific
frequencies (or wavelengths). Each element has a unique characteristic
line spectrum. Conversely, when continuous white light passes through a
cool gas, the gas absorbs light at precisely the same frequencies that
it emits when excited, resulting in dark lines in the continuous
spectrum (absorption spectrum).

Classical physics, based on the model of electrons orbiting the nucleus,
could not explain these discrete spectra. According to classical
electromagnetism, an accelerating electron (like one in orbit) should
continuously radiate energy, causing it to spiral into the nucleus, and
producing a continuous spectrum of radiation---neither of which is
observed. Atoms are stable, and their spectra are discrete. The spectrum
of hydrogen, the simplest atom, showed remarkable regularity (e.g., the
Balmer series in the visible region), described empirically by the
Rydberg formula, but lacked a theoretical explanation.

## Early Quantum Ideas: Bohr's Model {#ssec:bohr_model}

In 1913, Niels Bohr proposed a model for the hydrogen atom that, while
semi-classical, successfully explained its discrete spectrum by
incorporating Planck's quantum ideas.

### Quantization of Angular Momentum {#sssec:quantization_angular_momentum}

Bohr postulated that the electron could only exist in certain stable,
non-radiating circular orbits around the nucleus. These allowed orbits
were characterized by a condition quantizing the electron's orbital
angular momentum ($L$). He proposed that the angular momentum must be an
integer multiple of $\hbar= h / 2\pi$ (read \"h-bar\"):
$$L = m_e v r = n \hbar, \quad n = 1, 2, 3, \dots    \label{eq:bohr_quantization}$$
where $m_e$ is the electron mass, $v$ is its orbital speed, $r$ is the
orbit's radius, and $n$ is an integer called the principal quantum
number.

### Derivation of Quantized Energy Levels {#sssec:derivation_energy_levels}

Bohr combined this quantum condition with classical mechanics. For an
electron (charge $-e$) orbiting a proton (charge $+e$), the
electrostatic Coulomb force provides the necessary centripetal force for
a circular orbit:
$$\frac{k_e e^2}{r^2} = \frac{m_e v^2}{r}    \label{eq:coulomb_centripetal}$$
where $k_e = 1 / (4\pi\epsilon_0)$ is Coulomb's constant in SI units.
The total energy $E$ is the sum of kinetic energy
$K = \frac{1}{2} m_e v^2$ and potential energy $V = -k_e e^2 / r$:
$$E = K + V = \frac{1}{2} m_e v^2 - \frac{k_e e^2}{r}    \label{eq:classical_energy}$$
From
[\[eq:coulomb_centripetal\]](#eq:coulomb_centripetal){reference-type="ref+label"
reference="eq:coulomb_centripetal"}, we have $m_e v^2 = k_e e^2 / r$,
which implies $K = \frac{1}{2} (k_e e^2 / r) = -V/2$. Therefore, the
total energy can be expressed solely in terms of the radius:
$E = K + V = -V/2 + V = V/2 = -K$, specifically:
$$E = -\frac{1}{2} \frac{k_e e^2}{r}$$ By simultaneously solving the
force balance equation
[\[eq:coulomb_centripetal\]](#eq:coulomb_centripetal){reference-type="ref+label"
reference="eq:coulomb_centripetal"} and the angular momentum
quantization condition
[\[eq:bohr_quantization\]](#eq:bohr_quantization){reference-type="ref+label"
reference="eq:bohr_quantization"}, Bohr derived expressions for the
allowed radii $r_n$ and the corresponding allowed energy levels $E_n$:
$$\begin{aligned}    r_n &= \frac{n^2 \hbar^2}{m_e k_e e^2} = n^2 a_0 \\    E_n &= -\frac{1}{2} \frac{k_e e^2}{r_n} = -\left(\frac{m_e k_e^2 e^4}{2 \hbar^2}\right) \frac{1}{n^2} = -\frac{E_R}{n^2}    \label{eq:bohr_energy}\end{aligned}$$
Here, $a_0 = \hbar^2 / (m_e k_e e^2) \approx 0.0529$ nm is the Bohr
radius (radius of the $n=1$ orbit), and
$E_R = m_e k_e^2 e^4 / (2 \hbar^2) \approx 13.6$ eV is the Rydberg
energy. The model predicted that the electron could only occupy orbits
with these specific radii and energies; all other energies were
forbidden.

### Explanation of the Hydrogen Spectrum {#sssec:explanation_hydrogen_spectrum}

Bohr further postulated that an electron could make a transition, or
\"quantum jump,\" from a higher energy orbit $E_i$ (with quantum number
$n_i$) to a lower energy orbit $E_f$ (with quantum number $n_f$) by
emitting a single photon. The energy of the emitted photon must equal
the energy difference between the initial and final states, conserving
energy: $$E_{\text{photon}} = h\nu = \frac{hc}{\lambda} = E_i - E_f$$
Substituting the allowed energy levels from
[\[eq:bohr_energy\]](#eq:bohr_energy){reference-type="ref+label"
reference="eq:bohr_energy"}:
$$h\nu = \left(-\frac{E_R}{n_i^2}\right) - \left(-\frac{E_R}{n_f^2}\right) = E_R \left( \frac{1}{n_f^2} - \frac{1}{n_i^2} \right)$$
This directly leads to a prediction for the frequency $\nu$ or the
inverse wavelength $1/\lambda$ (wavenumber) of the emitted spectral
lines:
$$\frac{1}{\lambda} = \frac{E_R}{hc} \left( \frac{1}{n_f^2} - \frac{1}{n_i^2} \right) = R_H \left( \frac{1}{n_f^2} - \frac{1}{n_i^2} \right)    \label{eq:rydberg_formula}$$
where $R_H = E_R / (hc)$ is the Rydberg constant. This formula, derived
from Bohr's postulates, precisely matched the empirical Rydberg formula
and accurately predicted the wavelengths of all observed spectral lines
of hydrogen (e.g., the Lyman series with $n_f=1$, Balmer series with
$n_f=2$, Paschen series with $n_f=3$, etc.) by choosing appropriate
integer values for $n_i$ and $n_f$ ($n_i > n_f$).

Bohr model. Energy levels are quantized (<span class="math inline">\(E_n
\propto -1/n^2\)</span>). Spectral lines correspond to photons emitted

::: remark
Bohr's model was a monumental achievement, providing the first
quantitative explanation for atomic spectra and introducing the concept
of quantized energy levels and quantum jumps
([3](#fig:bohr_levels){reference-type="ref+Label"
reference="fig:bohr_levels"}). However, it was fundamentally flawed:

-   It worked well only for hydrogen and hydrogen-like ions
    (single-electron systems).

-   It could not explain the relative intensities of spectral lines.

-   It failed to account for the splitting of spectral lines in magnetic
    fields (Zeeman effect).

-   It mixed classical and quantum concepts in an ad hoc manner, lacking
    a consistent theoretical foundation (e.g., why were only certain
    orbits stable?).

Despite its limitations, Bohr's model was a crucial stepping stone,
highlighting the necessity of quantization and paving the way for the
development of a complete theory of quantum mechanics over the next
decade.
:::

## Development of Modern Quantum Mechanics {#ssec:development_modern_qm}

The limitations of the Bohr model and the growing body of experimental
evidence spurred efforts to develop a more comprehensive and consistent
quantum theory. This culminated in the mid-1920s with the emergence of
two mathematically distinct but physically equivalent formulations.

### Heisenberg's Matrix Mechanics (1925) {#sssec:heisenberg}

Werner Heisenberg, collaborating with Max Born and Pascual Jordan,
formulated **matrix mechanics**. In this abstract approach, classical
physical quantities like position ($x$) and momentum ($p$) were replaced
by infinite-dimensional matrices ($\hat{X}, \hat{P}$). The dynamics of
the system were described by matrix equations analogous to classical
equations of motion. A key feature was the inherent non-commutativity of
these matrices, reflecting the physical reality that the order of
measurements can matter. Specifically, Heisenberg postulated the
canonical commutation relation:
$$[\hat{X}, \hat{P}] = \hat{X}\hat{P} - \hat{P}\hat{X} = i\hbar\hat{I}$$
where $\hat{I}$ is the identity matrix. Matrix mechanics provided a
rigorous framework for calculating quantized energy levels and
transition probabilities, successfully generalizing Bohr's results and
other quantization rules. However, its abstract mathematical nature made
it difficult for many physicists to grasp intuitively.

### Schrödinger's Wave Mechanics (1926) {#sssec:schrodinger}

Inspired by Louis de Broglie's hypothesis (1924) that particles like
electrons also exhibit wave-like properties (with wavelength
$\lambda = h/p$), Erwin Schrödinger developed **wave mechanics**. In
this formulation, the state of a quantum system (e.g., an electron) is
described by a complex-valued **wave function**, $\psi(x, t)$. This wave
function contains all possible information about the system and its
evolution in time is governed by the **Schrödinger equation**, a partial
differential equation. For a non-relativistic particle of mass $m$
moving in a potential $V(x, t)$, the equation is:
$$i\hbar\frac{\partial}{\partial t} \psi(x, t) = \left[ -\frac{\hbar^2}{2m} \frac{\partial^2}{\partial x^2} + V(x, t) \right] \psi(x, t) = \hat{H} \psi(x, t)    \label{eq:schrodinger_general_1d}$$
where $\hat{H}$ is the Hamiltonian operator, representing the total
energy of the system. Schrödinger showed that solving this equation for
the hydrogen atom yielded the same quantized energy levels as Bohr's
model, but from a more fundamental basis. The wave mechanics approach,
drawing analogies with classical waves, was often found more intuitive
and easier to work with mathematically for many problems.

### Equivalence and the Copenhagen Interpretation {#sssec:equivalence_copenhagen}

Schrödinger himself, along with others like Carl Eckart and Paul Dirac,
soon demonstrated that matrix mechanics and wave mechanics, despite
their different mathematical appearances, were entirely equivalent. They
represented different mathematical bases for the same underlying
physical theory. Dirac later developed a more abstract and powerful
formalism using bras and kets, unifying both approaches within the
framework of linear algebra on Hilbert spaces.

The physical interpretation of the wave function $\psi$ remained a
subject of debate until Max Born (1926) proposed that the square of its
magnitude, $|\psi(x, t)|^2$, represents the **probability density** of
finding the particle at position $x$ at time $t$. This probabilistic
interpretation became a cornerstone of the standard, or **Copenhagen
interpretation**, of quantum mechanics, developed primarily by Bohr,
Heisenberg, Born, Pauli, and Dirac. Key tenets of this interpretation
include:

-   **Wave-particle duality:** Quantum objects exhibit both wave-like
    and particle-like properties depending on the experimental context.

-   **Probabilistic outcomes:** Measurements generally yield
    probabilistic results, predictable only in terms of probabilities
    given by the wave function (Born rule).

-   **Uncertainty principle:** There are fundamental limits to the
    precision with which certain pairs of complementary variables (like
    position and momentum) can be simultaneously known.

-   **Measurement disturbance:** The act of measurement inevitably
    disturbs the quantum system, causing its wave function to
    \"collapse\" into a state corresponding to the measured outcome.

-   **Correspondence principle:** In the limit of large quantum numbers
    or macroscopic systems, quantum mechanics should reproduce the
    results of classical physics.

The development of matrix mechanics and wave mechanics, their
unification, and the establishment of the Copenhagen interpretation
during 1925-1927 marked the completion of the foundations of modern
non-relativistic quantum mechanics, the theory that underpins quantum
computing.

# Mathematical Formalism and Postulates {#sec:mathematical_formalism}

Modern quantum mechanics is formulated using the language of linear
algebra over complex numbers, specifically within the framework of
Hilbert spaces. This mathematical structure provides the foundation for
describing quantum states, observables, measurements, and dynamics.

## Hilbert Spaces and State Vectors {#ssec:hilbert_spaces}

The state space of a quantum system is postulated to be a Hilbert space,
which is a complex vector space equipped with an inner product, and
which is complete with respect to the norm induced by the inner product.

### State Vectors (Kets) {#sssec:state_vectors}

An instantaneous state of a quantum system is represented by a **state
vector**, typically denoted using Dirac's ket notation as
$|\psi\rangle$. This vector belongs to the system's Hilbert space
$\mathcal{H}$.

-   For systems with a finite number of discrete states (like a qubit),
    the Hilbert space is finite-dimensional. For a single qubit, the
    space is $\mathcal{H} = \mathbb{C}^2$, spanned by the basis vectors
    $|0\rangle$ and $|1\rangle$. A general state is
    $|\psi\rangle = \alpha|0\rangle + \beta|1\rangle$, where
    $\alpha, \beta \in \mathbb{C}$.

-   For systems with continuous degrees of freedom (like the position of
    a particle in 1D), the Hilbert space is infinite-dimensional. A
    common example is the space of square-integrable complex functions,
    $L^2(\mathbb{R})$, where states are represented by wave functions
    $\psi(x)$.

### Inner Product (Bra-Ket Notation) {#sssec:inner_product}

The Hilbert space is equipped with an inner product, denoted
$\langle\phi|\psi\rangle$, which takes two state vectors $|\phi\rangle$
and $|\psi\rangle$ and returns a complex number. It satisfies:

1.  Linearity in the second argument (ket):
    $\langle\phi|\alpha\psi_1 + \beta\psi_2\rangle = \alpha\langle\phi|\psi_1\rangle + \beta\langle\phi|\psi_2\rangle$.

2.  Conjugate symmetry:
    $\langle\phi|\psi\rangle = (\langle\psi|\phi\rangle)^*$. (This
    implies anti-linearity in the first argument:
    $\langle\alpha\phi_1 + \beta\phi_2|\psi\rangle = \alpha^*\langle\phi_1|\psi\rangle + \beta^*\langle\phi_2|\psi\rangle$).

3.  Positive-definiteness: $\langle\psi|\psi\rangle \ge 0$, with
    equality if and only if $|\psi\rangle = \mathbf{0}$ (the zero
    vector).

The inner product allows us to define orthogonality
($\langle\phi|\psi\rangle = 0$) and the norm (or length) of a state
vector: $\|| \psi \rangle \| = \sqrt{\langle\psi|\psi\rangle}$.

Dirac notation introduces \"bra\" vectors $\langle\phi|$, which are
elements of the dual space $\mathcal{H}^*$ (the space of linear
functionals mapping kets to complex numbers). There is a one-to-one
correspondence between kets $|\phi\rangle$ and bras $\langle\phi|$, such
that applying the bra $\langle\phi|$ to the ket $|\psi\rangle$ yields
the inner product $\langle\phi|\psi\rangle$. In matrix representation
for finite dimensions, if $|\psi\rangle$ is a column vector,
$\langle\psi|$ is the correspondingconjugate transpose row vector
($\langle\psi| = |\psi\rangle^\dagger$).

-   For $|\psi\rangle, |\phi\rangle \in \mathbb{C}^d$ (column vectors):
    $\langle\phi|\psi\rangle = |\phi\rangle^\dagger |\psi\rangle = \sum_{i=1}^d \phi_i^* \psi_i$.

-   For wave functions $\psi(x), \phi(x) \in L^2(\mathbb{R})$:
    $\langle\phi|\psi\rangle = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \phi^*(x) \psi(x) dx$.

### Orthonormal Bases and Completeness {#sssec:orthonormal_bases}

A set of vectors $\{|u_i\rangle\}$ forms an **orthonormal basis** for
$\mathcal{H}$ if they are mutually orthogonal and normalized to unit
length:
$$\langle u_i | u_j \rangle = \delta_{ij} \quad (\text{Kronecker delta})$$
and if any vector $|\psi\rangle \in \mathcal{H}$ can be expressed as a
linear combination of these basis vectors:
$$|\psi\rangle = \sum_i c_i |u_i\rangle$$ The coefficients $c_i$ are
complex numbers given by the projection of $|\psi\rangle$ onto the basis
vector $|u_i\rangle$: $$c_i = \langle u_i | \psi \rangle$$ Substituting
this back gives
$|\psi\rangle = \sum_i \langle u_i | \psi \rangle |u_i\rangle = \sum_i |u_i\rangle \langle u_i | \psi \rangle$.
Since this holds for any $|\psi\rangle$, we arrive at the **completeness
relation** or **resolution of the identity**:
$$\sum_i |u_i\rangle \langle u_i| = \hat{I}    \label{eq:completeness}$$
where $\hat{I}$ is the identity operator on $\mathcal{H}$. The term
$|u_i\rangle \langle u_i|$ is an **outer product**, representing a
projection operator onto the state $|u_i\rangle$.

## Postulates of Quantum Mechanics {#ssec:postulates}

The behavior of quantum systems is governed by a set of fundamental
postulates.

::: postulate
[]{#post:state_space label="post:state_space"} Associated with any
isolated physical system is a complex vector space with an inner product
(i.e., a Hilbert space $\mathcal{H}$) known as the state space of the
system. The system is completely described by its state vector, which is
a unit vector in its state space.
:::

::: remark
The requirement that the state vector $|\psi\rangle$ be a unit vector
means $\langle\psi|\psi\rangle = 1$. This reflects the probabilistic
interpretation (total probability must be 1). Furthermore, state vectors
differing only by a global phase factor $e^{i\theta}$ (where
$\theta \in \mathbb{R}$) represent the same physical state, i.e.,
$|\psi\rangle$ and $e^{i\theta}|\psi\rangle$ are physically
indistinguishable. This is because all physically measurable quantities
(probabilities, expectation values) depend on terms like
$|\langle\phi|\psi\rangle|^2$ or $\langle\psi|\hat{A}|\psi\rangle$,
which are unaffected by such a phase factor.
:::

::: example
The state space for a single qubit is the two-dimensional complex
Hilbert space $\mathcal{H} = \mathbb{C}^2$. A standard orthonormal basis
is the computational basis $\{|0\rangle, |1\rangle\}$, where
$$|0\rangle = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}, \quad |1\rangle = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}$$
A general state vector is
$|\psi\rangle = \alpha|0\rangle + \beta|1\rangle = \begin{pmatrix} \alpha \\ \beta \end{pmatrix}$,
where $\alpha, \beta \in \mathbb{C}$. The normalization condition
requires $|\alpha|^2 + |\beta|^2 = 1$. States like
$\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(|0\rangle + |1\rangle)$ or
$\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(|0\rangle - i|1\rangle)$ are valid qubit states
representing superpositions.
:::

::: postulate
[]{#post:observables label="post:observables"} With every physically
measurable quantity $A$ (an observable), there is associated a Hermitian
operator $\hat{A}$ acting on the state space $\mathcal{H}$. The possible
outcomes of a measurement of $A$ are the eigenvalues of $\hat{A}$.
:::

::: definition
An operator $\hat{A}$ is **Hermitian** (or self-adjoint) if it is equal
to its adjoint (conjugate transpose): $\hat{A} = \hat{A}^\dagger$. Key
properties of Hermitian operators:

-   Their eigenvalues are always real numbers.

-   Their eigenvectors corresponding to distinct eigenvalues are
    orthogonal.

-   Their eigenvectors can always form an orthonormal basis for the
    Hilbert space $\mathcal{H}$ (Spectral Theorem).

If $\hat{A}|a_n\rangle = a_n |a_n\rangle$, then $a_n$ are the real
eigenvalues (possible measurement outcomes) and $|a_n\rangle$ are the
corresponding eigenvectors (states associated with those outcomes). The
set $\{|a_n\rangle\}$ forms an orthonormal basis satisfying
$\langle a_m | a_n \rangle = \delta_{mn}$ and
$\sum_n |a_n\rangle \langle a_n| = \hat{I}$.
:::

::: example
Important observables for a single qubit are represented by the Pauli
operators:
$$\begin{aligned}    \sigma_x = \hat{X} &= \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix} \quad (\text{Bit flip}) \\    \sigma_y = \hat{Y} &= \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -i \\ i & 0 \end{pmatrix} \\    \sigma_z = \hat{Z} &= \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & -1 \end{pmatrix} \quad (\text{Phase flip})\end{aligned}$$
These are all Hermitian matrices. Their eigenvalues are $+1$ and $-1$.

-   For $\hat{Z}$: Eigenvalues are $+1, -1$. Eigenvectors are
    $|0\rangle$ (for $+1$) and $|1\rangle$ (for $-1$). Measuring
    $\hat{Z}$ corresponds to measuring the qubit in the computational
    basis.

-   For $\hat{X}$: Eigenvalues are $+1, -1$. Eigenvectors are
    $|+\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(|0\rangle + |1\rangle)$ (for $+1$)
    and $|-\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(|0\rangle - |1\rangle)$ (for
    $-1$). Measuring $\hat{X}$ corresponds to measuring in the Hadamard
    basis $\{|+\rangle, |-\rangle\}$.
:::

::: postulate
[]{#post:measurement label="post:measurement"} If a measurement of the
observable $A$ (corresponding to operator $\hat{A}$) is performed on a
system in the normalized state $|\psi\rangle$, the probability of
obtaining the eigenvalue $a_n$ (assuming it is non-degenerate for
simplicity) is given by:
$$P(a_n) = |\langle a_n | \psi \rangle|^2    \label{eq:born_rule}$$
where $|a_n\rangle$ is the normalized eigenvector of $\hat{A}$
corresponding to the eigenvalue $a_n$. If the measurement yields the
result $a_n$, the state of the system immediately after the measurement
collapses to the corresponding eigenvector $|a_n\rangle$.

If the eigenvalue $a_n$ is degenerate, let
$\hat{P}_n = \sum_{i} |a_{n,i}\rangle \langle a_{n,i}|$ be the
projection operator onto the eigenspace corresponding to $a_n$ (where
$\{|a_{n,i}\rangle\}$ is an orthonormal basis for that eigenspace). Then
the probability of measuring $a_n$ is:
$$P(a_n) = \langle \psi | \hat{P}_n | \psi \rangle = \sum_i |\langle a_{n,i} | \psi \rangle|^2    \label{eq:born_rule_degenerate}$$
And the state after measurement collapses to:
$$|\psi'\rangle = \frac{\hat{P}_n |\psi\rangle}{\sqrt{\langle \psi | \hat{P}_n | \psi \rangle}}    \label{eq:collapse_degenerate}$$
:::

::: remark
The average value, or **expectation value**, obtained from many
measurements of $A$ on identically prepared systems in state
$|\psi\rangle$ is:
$$\langle \hat{A} \rangle = \sum_n a_n P(a_n) = \sum_n a_n |\langle a_n | \psi \rangle|^2$$
Using the spectral decomposition
$\hat{A} = \sum_n a_n |a_n\rangle \langle a_n|$ and the completeness
relation, this can be written more compactly as:
$$\langle \hat{A} \rangle = \langle \psi | \hat{A} | \psi \rangle    \label{eq:expectation_value}$$
:::

::: postulate
[]{#post:time_evolution label="post:time_evolution"} The evolution of a
closed quantum system is described by the **Schrödinger equation**:
$$i\hbar\frac{d}{dt} |\psi(t)\rangle = \hat{H} |\psi(t)\rangle    \label{eq:schrodinger_abstract}$$
where $\hbar= h/2\pi$ is the reduced Planck constant, and $\hat{H}$ is
the **Hamiltonian** operator of the system, which corresponds to the
total energy observable.

If the Hamiltonian $\hat{H}$ is time-independent, the solution to the
Schrödinger equation is:
$$|\psi(t)\rangle = \hat{U}(t, t_0) |\psi(t_0)\rangle = e^{-i\hat{H}(t-t_0)/\hbar} |\psi(t_0)\rangle    \label{eq:time_evolution_operator}$$
where $\hat{U}(t, t_0) = e^{-i\hat{H}(t-t_0)/\hbar}$ is the **time
evolution operator**. This operator is **unitary**, meaning
$\hat{U}^\dagger \hat{U} = \hat{U} \hat{U}^\dagger = \hat{I}$. Unitary
evolution preserves the inner product between states and, consequently,
the normalization of state vectors. Quantum computation is primarily
concerned with implementing specific sequences of unitary
transformations (quantum gates) on qubit states.
:::

::: remark
Quantum mechanics describes two distinct types of state evolution:

1.  **Unitary evolution:** Deterministic, continuous, and reversible
    evolution of an isolated system according to the Schrödinger
    equation
    ([\[post:time_evolution\]](#post:time_evolution){reference-type="ref+Label"
    reference="post:time_evolution"}). This is how quantum states evolve
    between measurements or interactions with the environment. Quantum
    gates perform unitary evolution.

2.  **Measurement:** Probabilistic, discontinuous, and irreversible
    change in the state upon interaction with a measurement apparatus
    ([\[post:measurement\]](#post:measurement){reference-type="ref+Label"
    reference="post:measurement"}). This \"collapse\" projects the state
    onto one of the eigenstates of the measured observable.

The interplay between these two processes is fundamental to quantum
mechanics and quantum computation.
:::

::: postulate
[]{#post:composite_systems label="post:composite_systems"} The state
space of a composite physical system is the tensor product of the state
spaces of the individual component systems. If system 1 is described by
Hilbert space $\mathcal{H}_1$ and system 2 by $\mathcal{H}_2$, the
composite system is described by the Hilbert space
$\mathcal{H} = \mathcal{H}_1 \otimes \mathcal{H}_2$. If system 1 is in
state $|\psi_1\rangle \in \mathcal{H}_1$ and system 2 is in state
$|\psi_2\rangle \in \mathcal{H}_2$, the state of the composite system is
$|\psi_1\rangle \otimes |\psi_2\rangle$, often written as
$|\psi_1\rangle |\psi_2\rangle$ or $|\psi_1, \psi_2\rangle$.
:::

::: remark
The dimension of the composite Hilbert space is the product of the
dimensions of the component spaces:
$\dim(\mathcal{H}_1 \otimes \mathcal{H}_2) = \dim(\mathcal{H}_1) \times \dim(\mathcal{H}_2)$.
A crucial consequence of the tensor product structure is the existence
of **entangled states**. These are states of a composite system that
cannot be written as a simple product state
$|\psi_1\rangle \otimes |\psi_2\rangle$. For example, the Bell state
$|\Phi^+\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(|00\rangle + |11\rangle)$ in
$\mathbb{C}^2 \otimes \mathbb{C}^2$ is entangled. Entanglement
represents correlations between quantum systems that are stronger than
any possible classical correlation and is a key resource in quantum
computation and communication.
:::

::: example
The state space for two qubits is
$\mathcal{H} = \mathbb{C}^2 \otimes \mathbb{C}^2 = \mathbb{C}^4$. A
basis for this space is formed by the tensor products of the
single-qubit basis states:
$$\begin{aligned}    |00\rangle &= |0\rangle \otimes |0\rangle = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix} \otimes \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix} \\    |01\rangle &= |0\rangle \otimes |1\rangle = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix} \otimes \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix} \\    |10\rangle &= |1\rangle \otimes |0\rangle = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix} \otimes \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix} \\    |11\rangle &= |1\rangle \otimes |1\rangle = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix} \otimes \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ 0 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}\end{aligned}$$
A general two-qubit state is
$|\psi\rangle = c_{00}|00\rangle + c_{01}|01\rangle + c_{10}|10\rangle + c_{11}|11\rangle$,
with $\sum_{ij} |c_{ij}|^2 = 1$. The state
$\frac{1}{2}(|00\rangle + |01\rangle + |10\rangle + |11\rangle)$ is a
product state:
$\left(\frac{|0\rangle+|1\rangle}{\sqrt{2}}\right) \otimes \left(\frac{|0\rangle+|1\rangle}{\sqrt{2}}\right) = |+\rangle \otimes |+\rangle$.
The state $|\Phi^+\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(|00\rangle + |11\rangle)$
is entangled.
:::

These postulates form the bedrock upon which the theory of quantum
mechanics is built, providing the rules for describing quantum states,
predicting measurement outcomes, and understanding how quantum systems
evolve and combine. They are the essential tools needed to analyze and
design quantum algorithms and understand the workings of quantum
computers.
