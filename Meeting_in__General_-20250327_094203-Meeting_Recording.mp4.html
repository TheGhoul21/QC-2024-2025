Introduction

This document provides structured lecture notes covering fundamental concepts from quantum mechanics that are essential for understanding the nature and implementation of quantum bits (qubits). The primary objective is to connect the abstract principles of quantum theory to their concrete realization in physical systems, thereby laying the groundwork for comprehending how quantum computation leverages these phenomena.

We commence by examining the Quantum Harmonic Oscillator (QHO). This model is not only one of the simplest solvable systems in quantum mechanics but also universally relevant, as many physical systems near stable equilibrium points can be approximated as harmonic oscillators. We will focus on its characteristic quantized energy levels and discuss their suitability for encoding quantum information.

Subsequently, the notes delve into the concept of angular momentum within the quantum framework. This includes the quantization rules governing both orbital angular momentum and the intrinsic angular momentum known as spin. The counter-intuitive nature of spin quantization, experimentally verified by the Stern-Gerlach experiment, will be highlighted as a key resource for qubit development.

Following this foundational physics, we explore specific physical systems commonly employed for qubit implementation, such as the spin states of electrons and the polarization states of photons. A crucial aspect discussed is the process of abstracting the simplified two-level qubit description (the computational basis states \(|0\rangle\) and \(|1\rangle\)) from the more intricate state description of the full physical system, which inherently includes spatial degrees of freedom (wavefunctions) and temporal evolution dynamics. This abstraction is vital for focusing on the information-carrying aspects relevant to computation.

Finally, the core principles governing qubit behavior are reviewed: the representation of states using complex amplitudes in a Hilbert space, the concept of superposition allowing simultaneous existence in multiple basis states, the probabilistic nature of measurement outcomes governed by the Born rule, and the evolution of quantum states described by unitary operators. These principles form the bedrock of quantum information processing. This lecture aims to equip the reader with the necessary physics background to appreciate the construction, manipulation, and underlying principles of quantum computing devices.

The Quantum Harmonic Oscillator

The Quantum Harmonic Oscillator (QHO) serves as a fundamental and exactly solvable model in quantum mechanics. It describes a system experiencing a restoring force proportional to its displacement from a stable equilibrium position, analogous to a mass attached to an ideal spring obeying Hooke’s law. Its importance extends far beyond this simple picture, providing a basis for understanding phenomena ranging from molecular vibrations to the quantization of fields.

Classical Analogue and Significance

Classically, the harmonic oscillator is characterized by a potential energy function that is quadratic in the displacement \(x\) from equilibrium: \[V(x) = \frac{1}{2} k x^2 = \frac{1}{2} m \omega_0^2 x^2. \label{eq:classical_potential}\] Here, \(k\) represents the spring constant, \(m\) is the mass of the oscillating particle, and \(\omega_0 = \sqrt{k/m}\) is the natural angular frequency of oscillation. The force exerted on the particle is \(F(x) = -\frac{dV}{dx} = -kx\), always directed towards the equilibrium point \(x=0\).

Remark. Reduced Massrem:reduced_mass In systems involving two interacting bodies (e.g., a diatomic molecule), the analysis of relative motion often leads to an equivalent one-body problem. In such cases, the mass \(m\) in [eq:classical_potential] is replaced by the reduced mass \(\mu = \frac{m_1 m_2}{m_1 + m_2}\), where \(m_1\) and \(m_2\) are the masses of the two bodies.

The harmonic oscillator model is remarkably pervasive in physics for several reasons:

  • Approximation near Equilibrium: As highlighted in the lecture, any physical system near a point of stable equilibrium (a minimum of its potential energy \(V\)) behaves approximately like a harmonic oscillator for small displacements. A Taylor expansion of \(V(x)\) around the minimum \(x_0\) yields: \[V(x) \approx V(x_0) + \underbrace{V'(x_0)}_{=0 \text{ at min}} (x-x_0) + \frac{1}{2} \underbrace{V''(x_0)}_{=k > 0} (x-x_0)^2 + \mathcal{O}((x-x_0)^3). \label{eq:taylor_expansion}\] Ignoring the constant energy offset \(V(x_0)\) and higher-order terms, the potential is quadratic, corresponding to harmonic oscillation with an effective spring constant \(k = V''(x_0)\).

  • Fundamental Excitations: The QHO framework is essential for describing fundamental excitations in various physical systems, including:

    • Vibrations of atoms in molecules.

    • Lattice vibrations in crystalline solids (phonons).

    • Quantization of the electromagnetic field (photons), where each mode of the field behaves as an independent QHO.

Quantum Hamiltonian and Energy Quantization

To transition to quantum mechanics, we promote the classical position \(x\) and momentum \(p\) to operators \(\hat{x}\) and \(\hat{p}\), respectively. In the position representation, \(\hat{x} = x\) and \(\hat{p} = -i\hbar\frac{d}{dx}\). These operators satisfy the canonical commutation relation \([\hat{x}, \hat{p}] = i\hbar\). The Hamiltonian operator for the one-dimensional QHO is obtained by substituting these operators into the classical Hamiltonian function \(H = p^2/(2m) + V(x)\): \[\hat{H} = \frac{\hat{p}^2}{2m} + \frac{1}{2} m \omega_0^2 \hat{x}^2 = -\frac{\hbar^2}{2m} \frac{d^2}{dx^2} + \frac{1}{2} m \omega_0^2 x^2. \label{eq:qho_hamiltonian}\] The energy eigenstates \(|\psi_n\rangle\) and corresponding energy eigenvalues \(E_n\) are found by solving the time-independent Schrödinger equation: \[\hat{H} |\psi_n\rangle = E_n |\psi_n\rangle.\] The solutions reveal that the energy spectrum is discrete (quantized).

Energy Eigenvalues of the QHOthm:qho_energy This theorem specifies the discrete energy levels allowed for a quantum harmonic oscillator.

The allowed energy levels for the one-dimensional quantum harmonic oscillator are given by: \[E_n = \left(n + \frac{1}{2}\right) \hbar\omega_0 = \left(n + \frac{1}{2}\right) h \nu_0, \quad \text{for } n = 0, 1, 2, \dots \label{eq:qho_energy}\] where \(\nu_0 = \omega_0 / (2\pi)\) is the natural frequency, \(h = 2\pi \hbar\) is Planck’s constant, and \(n\) is the energy level quantum number.

Remark. Properties of QHO Energy Levelsrem:qho_levels

  • Quantization: Only discrete energy values \(E_n\) are permitted, in contrast to the continuous range of energies allowed classically.

  • Equispaced Levels: The energy levels are uniformly spaced, with a constant energy difference between adjacent levels: \[\Delta E = E_{n+1} - E_n = \hbar\omega_0 = h \nu_0. \label{eq:energy_spacing}\] This means that transitions between adjacent levels always involve the absorption or emission of the same quantum of energy, \(\hbar\omega_0\).

  • Zero-Point Energy: The lowest possible energy state (ground state), corresponding to \(n=0\), has a non-zero energy \(E_0 = \frac{1}{2} \hbar\omega_0\). This "zero-point energy" is a fundamental consequence of the Heisenberg uncertainty principle; the particle cannot be perfectly localized at the potential minimum with zero momentum simultaneously.

Ladder Operator Formalism

An elegant and powerful method for analyzing the QHO involves the introduction of non-Hermitian ladder operators, the annihilation operator \(\hat{a}\) and the creation operator \(\hat{a}^\dagger\): \[\begin{aligned} \hat{a} &= \sqrt{\frac{m\omega_0}{2\hbar}} \left( \hat{x} + \frac{i}{m\omega_0} \hat{p} \right) \label{eq:annihilation_op} \\ \hat{a}^\dagger &= \sqrt{\frac{m\omega_0}{2\hbar}} \left( \hat{x} - \frac{i}{m\omega_0} \hat{p} \right) \label{eq:creation_op}\end{aligned}\] These operators satisfy the fundamental commutation relation: \[[\hat{a}, \hat{a}^\dagger] = \hat{a}\hat{a}^\dagger - \hat{a}^\dagger\hat{a} = 1. \label{eq:ladder_commutation}\] In terms of these operators, the position and momentum operators can be expressed as: \[\begin{aligned} \hat{x} &= \sqrt{\frac{\hbar}{2m\omega_0}} (\hat{a} + \hat{a}^\dagger) \\ \hat{p} &= i \sqrt{\frac{m\omega_0\hbar}{2}} (\hat{a}^\dagger - \hat{a})\end{aligned}\] Crucially, the Hamiltonian ([eq:qho_hamiltonian]) takes a very simple form: \[\hat{H} = \hbar\omega_0 \left( \hat{a}^\dagger \hat{a} + \frac{1}{2} \right). \label{eq:hamiltonian_ladder}\] The operator \(\hat{N} = \hat{a}^\dagger \hat{a}\) is called the number operator. Its eigenvalues are the non-negative integers \(n=0, 1, 2, \dots\), confirming the energy spectrum \(E_n = \hbar\omega_0 (n + 1/2)\). The eigenstates \(|n\rangle\) are simultaneous eigenstates of \(\hat{H}\) and \(\hat{N}\).

The names "annihilation" and "creation" stem from their effect on the energy eigenstates \(|n\rangle\): \[\begin{aligned} \hat{a} |n\rangle &= \sqrt{n} |n-1\rangle \quad (\text{for } n \ge 1), \quad \hat{a} |0\rangle = 0 \\ \hat{a}^\dagger |n\rangle &= \sqrt{n+1} |n+1\rangle\end{aligned}\] The annihilation operator \(\hat{a}\) lowers the energy level by one quantum \(\hbar\omega_0\) (destroying one energy quantum), while the creation operator \(\hat{a}^\dagger\) raises the energy level by one quantum (creating one energy quantum). This formalism directly demonstrates the equispaced nature of the energy levels and provides a simple way to construct all excited states starting from the ground state \(|0\rangle\) (defined by \(\hat{a}|0\rangle=0\)) via repeated application of \(\hat{a}^\dagger\).

Potential for Qubit Encoding

The discrete and equally spaced energy levels of the QHO provide a natural ladder for encoding information. Specifically, the two lowest energy states can be chosen to represent the computational basis states of a qubit.

Qubit Encoding in QHOex:qho_qubit A common encoding scheme identifies: \[\begin{aligned} |0\rangle_{\text{qubit}} &\equiv |n=0\rangle \quad (\text{Ground state, Energy } E_0 = \frac{1}{2} \hbar\omega_0) \\ |1\rangle_{\text{qubit}} &\equiv |n=1\rangle \quad (\text{First excited state, Energy } E_1 = \frac{3}{2} \hbar\omega_0) \end{aligned}\] A general qubit state \(|\psi\rangle = \alpha|0\rangle_{\text{qubit}} + \beta|1\rangle_{\text{qubit}}\) then corresponds to a quantum superposition of the oscillator being in its ground and first excited states. Transitions between \(|0\rangle\) andA general qubit state \(|\psi\rangle = \alpha|0\rangle_{\text{qubit}} + \beta|1\rangle_{\text{qubit}}\) then corresponds to a quantum superposition of the oscillator being in its ground and first excited states. Transitions between \(|0\rangle\) and \(|1\rangle\) can be driven by applying an external field (e.g., electromagnetic radiation) resonant with the energy difference \(\Delta E = E_1 - E_0 = \hbar\omega_0\).

Remark. Requirements and Considerations for Qubit Encodingrem:qho_qubit_reqs

  • Addressability: To function as a qubit, one must be able to selectively induce transitions between the chosen \(|0\rangle\) and \(|1\rangle\) states without significantly populating higher energy states (\(|n=2\rangle, |n=3\rangle, \dots\)).

  • Anharmonicity: Real physical systems that approximate harmonic oscillators (e.g., molecular bonds, superconducting circuits) often exhibit *anharmonicity*. This means the potential \(V(x)\) deviates from the perfect quadratic form at larger displacements, causing the energy level spacing to become non-uniform. For instance, \(E_2 - E_1\) might differ slightly from \(E_1 - E_0\). While this deviates from the ideal QHO model, anharmonicity can be advantageous for qubit encoding. A slightly smaller gap between \(E_1\) and \(E_2\) than between \(E_0\) and \(E_1\) allows driving frequencies to be tuned to specifically address the \(0 \leftrightarrow 1\) transition without exciting the \(1 \leftrightarrow 2\) transition, improving qubit control fidelity.

  • Temperature: Thermal energy (\(k_B T\)) must typically be kept much smaller than the energy gap \(\hbar\omega_0\) (\(k_B T \ll \hbar\omega_0\)) to prevent unwanted thermal excitations out of the qubit subspace. This often necessitates operation at cryogenic temperatures.

  • General Principle: The use of energy levels for qubit encoding is a general feature of confined quantum systems. Any system exhibiting discrete energy levels due to confinement (e.g., electrons in atoms, particles in quantum wells) can potentially host qubits, provided suitable levels can be isolated and controlled.

Angular Momentum in Quantum Mechanics

Angular momentum, a measure of rotational motion, is a fundamental conserved quantity in classical physics. In quantum mechanics, it retains its importance but exhibits distinct, quantized properties that are crucial for understanding atomic structure and provide key resources for quantum information processing, particularly through the concept of spin.

Classical Definition and Quantum Operators

Classically, the orbital angular momentum \(\mathbf{L}\) of a particle with position vector \(\mathbf{r}\) and linear momentum \(\mathbf{p}\) is defined by the vector product: \[\mathbf{L} = \mathbf{r} \times \mathbf{p}.\] The Cartesian components are given by: \[\begin{aligned} L_x &= y p_z - z p_y, \\ L_y &= z p_x - x p_z, \\ L_z &= x p_y - y p_x.\end{aligned}\] Following the standard quantization procedure, we replace the classical position and momentum components with their corresponding quantum operators: \(\hat{x}, \hat{y}, \hat{z}\) and \(\hat{p}_x = -i\hbar\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\), \(\hat{p}_y = -i\hbar\frac{\partial}{\partial y}\), \(\hat{p}_z = -i\hbar\frac{\partial}{\partial z}\). This yields the quantum mechanical orbital angular momentum operators: \[\begin{aligned} \hat{L}_x &= \hat{y} \hat{p}_z - \hat{z} \hat{p}_y = -i\hbar\left( y \frac{\partial}{\partial z} - z \frac{\partial}{\partial y} \right), \label{eq:Lx_op} \\ \hat{L}_y &= \hat{z} \hat{p}_x - \hat{x} \hat{p}_z = -i\hbar\left( z \frac{\partial}{\partial x} - x \frac{\partial}{\partial z} \right), \label{eq:Ly_op} \\ \hat{L}_z &= \hat{x} \hat{p}_y - \hat{y} \hat{p}_x = -i\hbar\left( x \frac{\partial}{\partial y} - y \frac{\partial}{\partial x} \right). \label{eq:Lz_op}\end{aligned}\] A fundamental property of these operators is their non-commutativity. They obey the cyclic commutation relations: \[[\hat{L}_x, \hat{L}_y] = i\hbar\hat{L}_z, \quad [\hat{L}_y, \hat{L}_z] = i\hbar\hat{L}_x, \quad [\hat{L}_z, \hat{L}_x] = i\hbar\hat{L}_y. \label{eq:L_commutation}\] These can be summarized compactly using the Levi-Civita symbol \(\epsilon_{ijk}\): \([\hat{L}_i, \hat{L}_j] = i\hbar\sum_{k} \epsilon_{ijk} \hat{L}_k\). The non-commutativity implies, via the generalized uncertainty principle, that it is impossible to simultaneously determine the precise values of more than one component of the angular momentum vector.

However, the square of the total angular momentum operator, defined as \[\hat{L}^2 = \hat{L}_x^2 + \hat{L}_y^2 + \hat{L}_z^2,\] commutes with each individual component: \[[\hat{L}^2, \hat{L}_i] = 0 \quad \text{for } i = x, y, z. \label{eq:L2_commutation}\] This crucial property allows us to find simultaneous eigenstates of \(\hat{L}^2\) and one component, conventionally chosen as \(\hat{L}_z\). These eigenstates form the basis for describing the angular momentum state of a quantum system.

Quantization of Angular Momentum Eigenvalues

The algebraic structure defined by the commutation relations [eq:L_commutation,eq:L2_commutation] dictates the possible eigenvalues of \(\hat{L}^2\) and \(\hat{L}_z\).

Angular Momentum Eigenvaluesthm:am_eigenvalues This theorem establishes the quantized values that the square of the total angular momentum (\(\hat{L}^2\)) and one of its components (conventionally \(\hat{L}_z\)) can take in quantum mechanics, based on the commutation relations of the angular momentum operators.

The simultaneous eigenstates of \(\hat{L}^2\) and \(\hat{L}_z\), denoted by \(|l, m\rangle\), satisfy the eigenvalue equations: \[\begin{aligned} \hat{L}^2 |l, m\rangle &= \hbar^2 l(l+1) |l, m\rangle, \label{eq:L2_eigenvalue} \\ \hat{L}_z |l, m\rangle &= \hbar m |l, m\rangle. \label{eq:Lz_eigenvalue} \end{aligned}\] The quantum numbers \(l\) and \(m\) are restricted to the following values:

  • \(l\) is the azimuthal or orbital angular momentum quantum number. For orbital angular momentum derived from \(\mathbf{r} \times \mathbf{p}\), \(l\) must be a non-negative integer: \(l = 0, 1, 2, \dots\).

  • \(m\) is the magnetic quantum number. For a given value of \(l\), \(m\) can take on \((2l+1)\) integer values, ranging from \(-l\) to \(+l\) in integer steps: \(m = -l, -l+1, \dots, l-1, l\).

The states \(|l, m\rangle\) for fixed \(l\) and varying \(m\) form an orthonormal basis for the subspace associated with angular momentum \(l\).

Remark. Magnitude vs. Projection and the Vector Modelrem:am_vector_model

  • The eigenvalue of \(\hat{L}^2\) determines the square of the magnitude of the angular momentum vector. The magnitude itself is \(\left\|\mathbf{L}\right\| = \sqrt{\hbar^2 l(l+1)} = \hbar\sqrt{l(l+1)}\).

  • The eigenvalue of \(\hat{L}_z\) gives the projection of the angular momentum vector onto the z-axis, which is \(\hbar m\).

  • The maximum possible projection along the z-axis is \(\hbar l\). Since \(\sqrt{l(l+1)} > l\) for \(l > 0\), the magnitude of the angular momentum vector is always strictly greater than its maximum projection onto any single axis.

  • This leads to the "vector model" visualization: the angular momentum vector \(\mathbf{L}\) can be thought of as precessing around the z-axis, maintaining a constant magnitude \(\hbar\sqrt{l(l+1)}\) and a constant z-projection \(\hbar m\), but with indeterminate \(L_x\) and \(L_y\) components. The vector lies on the surface of a cone with height \(\hbar m\) and slant height \(\hbar\sqrt{l(l+1)}\).

Remark. Spherical Harmonicsrem:spherical_harmonics When working in the position representation (using spherical coordinates \(r, \theta, \phi\)), the simultaneous eigenstates \(\langle\theta, \phi|l, m\rangle\) are the spherical harmonics \(Y_{lm}(\theta, \phi)\). These functions describe the angular dependence of wavefunctions for systems with definite angular momentum, such as electron orbitals in atoms.

Spin as Intrinsic Angular Momentum

Experiments revealed that elementary particles like electrons possess an additional form of angular momentum that is intrinsic, not associated with their spatial motion (\(\mathbf{r} \times \mathbf{p}\)). This intrinsic angular momentum is called spin, denoted by \(\hat{\mathbf{S}}\).

Spin Angular Momentumdefn:spin Spin \(\hat{\mathbf{S}} = (\hat{S}_x, \hat{S}_y, \hat{S}_z)\) is an intrinsic angular momentum vector operator whose components obey the same commutation relations as orbital angular momentum: \[[\hat{S}_i, \hat{S}_j] = i\hbar\sum_{k} \epsilon_{ijk} \hat{S}_k.\] Similarly, the square of the spin operator \(\hat{S}^2 = \hat{S}_x^2 + \hat{S}_y^2 + \hat{S}_z^2\) commutes with its components: \([\hat{S}^2, \hat{S}_i] = 0\). The simultaneous eigenstates of \(\hat{S}^2\) and \(\hat{S}_z\), denoted \(|s, m_s\rangle\), satisfy: \[\begin{aligned} \hat{S}^2 |s, m_s\rangle &= \hbar^2 s(s+1) |s, m_s\rangle, \\ \hat{S}_z |s, m_s\rangle &= \hbar m_s |s, m_s\rangle. \end{aligned}\] Here, \(s\) is the spin quantum number, which is a fixed characteristic of a given type of particle. Unlike the orbital quantum number \(l\), \(s\) can be a non-negative integer or a half-integer (\(s = 0, 1/2, 1, 3/2, \dots\)). For a given \(s\), the spin magnetic quantum number \(m_s\) takes on \((2s+1)\) values: \(m_s = -s, -s+1, \dots, s-1, s\).

Electron Spin (Spin-1/2)ex:electron_spin Electrons, protons, and neutrons are fundamental examples of spin-1/2 particles (\(s=1/2\)).

  • The eigenvalue of \(\hat{S}^2\) is fixed: \(\hbar^2 s(s+1) = \hbar^2 (\frac{1}{2})(\frac{1}{2}+1) = \frac{3}{4} \hbar^2\).

  • The spin magnetic quantum number \(m_s\) can only take two values: \(m_s = -1/2\) and \(m_s = +1/2\).

  • The corresponding eigenvalues of \(\hat{S}_z\) are \(-\frac{\hbar}{2}\) and \(+\frac{\hbar}{2}\).

  • These two basis states represent the intrinsic spin degree of freedom of the electron. They are commonly denoted as:

    • \(|\downarrow\rangle\) or \(|s=1/2, m_s=-1/2\rangle\): "spin down" state.

    • \(|\uparrow\rangle\) or \(|s=1/2, m_s=+1/2\rangle\): "spin up" state.

  • This naturally forms a two-dimensional Hilbert space, making spin-1/2 systems ideal candidates for implementing qubits, where \(|0\rangle \equiv |\downarrow\rangle\) and \(|1\rangle \equiv |\uparrow\rangle\) (or vice versa).

Remark. Spin Measurement Outcomes: \(\pm 1\) vs \(\pm \hbar/2\)rem:spin_measurement_units The lecture mentions measuring spin projection and obtaining outcomes \(+1\) or \(-1\). This common convention arises from measuring the spin projection in units of \(\hbar/2\). That is, the observable being measured is effectively \(\frac{2}{\hbar} \hat{S}_z\), whose eigenvalues are \(2 m_s = 2(\pm 1/2) = \pm 1\). The fundamental eigenvalues of \(\hat{S}_z\) itself remain \(\pm \hbar/2\).

Remark. Pauli Matricesrem:pauli_matrices For spin-1/2 systems, the spin operators \(\hat{S}_x, \hat{S}_y, \hat{S}_z\) acting on the two-dimensional spin space \(\{|\uparrow\rangle, |\downarrow\rangle\}\) can be represented by matrices proportional to the Pauli matrices \(\sigma_x, \sigma_y, \sigma_z\): \[\hat{S}_i = \frac{\hbar}{2} \sigma_i,\] where \[\sigma_x = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}, \quad \sigma_y = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -i \\ i & 0 \end{pmatrix}, \quad \sigma_z = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & -1 \end{pmatrix}.\] These matrices are fundamental in the description and manipulation of qubits.

The Stern-Gerlach Experiment: Evidence for Spin Quantization

The Stern-Gerlach experiment, performed by Otto Stern and Walther Gerlach in 1922, provided the first direct experimental evidence for the quantization of angular momentum projection, later understood to be dominated by electron spin.

Setup:

  • A beam of neutral atoms (originally Silver, Ag) is produced in an oven and collimated. Silver atoms have a single valence electron in an s-orbital (\(l=0\)), so their total angular momentum is dominated by the spin of this electron (\(s=1/2\)).

  • The beam passes through a region with a strong inhomogeneous magnetic field, typically designed to have a significant gradient in one direction (e.g., \(\partial B_z / \partial z \neq 0\)).

  • A detection screen is placed after the magnet to observe the spatial distribution of the atoms.

Physics:

  • An atom with magnetic dipole moment \(\mathbf{\mu}\) in a magnetic field \(\mathbf{B}\) experiences a potential energy \(U = -\mathbf{\mu} \cdot \mathbf{B}\).

  • In an inhomogeneous field, this leads to a net force \(\mathbf{F} = -\nabla U = \nabla (\mathbf{\mu} \cdot \mathbf{B})\).

  • If the field is primarily \(B_z(z)\) and the gradient is \(\partial B_z / \partial z\), the dominant force is in the z-direction: \(F_z \approx \mu_z \frac{\partial B_z}{\partial z}\).

  • The magnetic moment \(\mathbf{\mu}\) is proportional to the total angular momentum \(\hat{\mathbf{J}}\) (orbital + spin). For Silver atoms in the ground state, \(\hat{\mathbf{L}}=0\), so \(\mathbf{\mu}\) is proportional to the electron spin \(\hat{\mathbf{S}}\). Specifically, \(\mathbf{\mu}_S = g_e \frac{-e}{2m_e} \hat{\mathbf{S}}\), where \(g_e \approx 2\) is the electron g-factor. Thus, \(\mu_z \propto S_z\).

  • The force \(F_z\) deflecting the atoms is therefore proportional to the eigenvalue of \(\hat{S}_z\).

Classical Expectation:

  • If angular momentum (and thus \(\mu_z\)) could take any continuous value corresponding to arbitrary orientations of the magnetic moment vector, the atoms would be deflected by varying amounts, resulting in a continuous smear on the detection screen.

Observed Result:

  • The beam splits into a discrete number of distinct spots. For Silver atoms (\(s=1/2\)), exactly two spots are observed, corresponding to deflections upwards and downwards.

Conclusion:

  • The experiment demonstrates the spatial quantization of angular momentum projection. The z-component of the angular momentum (in this case, spin) can only take on a discrete set of values.

  • For spin-1/2 particles, there are only two possible values for \(S_z\): \(+\hbar/2\) and \(-\hbar/2\), leading to the two observed beams. This was a radical departure from classical physics and a key confirmation of quantum theory.

Remark. Spin and Magnetic Moment Connectionrem:spin_mag_moment The lecture’s reference to the atom behaving like a small magnet ("calamita") directly relates to the magnetic dipole moment \(\mathbf{\mu}\) associated with the electron’s spin. It is the interaction of this intrinsic magnetic moment with the external magnetic field gradient that causes the force and subsequent deflection observed in the Stern-Gerlach experiment. The quantization of the deflection angles is direct proof of the quantization of the spin projection \(S_z\).

Physical Systems for Qubit Implementation

The abstract concept of a qubit, a two-level quantum system, finds concrete realization in various physical platforms. These systems leverage the inherent quantum properties discussed previously, such as quantized energy levels (2) and spin angular momentum (3), to encode and manipulate quantum information. We will explore some prominent examples, starting with electron spin and photon polarization, and discuss the crucial abstraction involved in isolating the qubit dynamics from the full system description.

Electron Spin Qubits

As established in 3.3, the intrinsic spin angular momentum of an electron provides a natural two-level system. Being a spin-1/2 particle, its spin projection along any chosen axis (conventionally the z-axis) is quantized to two possible values, \(m_s = \pm 1/2\).

Electron Spin Qubit Basisdefn:spin_qubit_basis The computational basis states \(|0\rangle\) and \(|1\rangle\) are typically identified with the spin-down and spin-up states, respectively: \[\begin{aligned} |0\rangle &\equiv |\downarrow\rangle \equiv |s=1/2, m_s=-1/2\rangle \\ |1\rangle &\equiv |\uparrow\rangle \equiv |s=1/2, m_s=+1/2\rangle \end{aligned}\] A general state is a superposition \(|\psi\rangle = \alpha |0\rangle + \beta |1\rangle\).

Interaction and Manipulation

The electron’s spin is associated with an intrinsic magnetic dipole moment \(\mathbf{\mu}_S = g_e \frac{-e}{2m_e} \hat{\mathbf{S}}\), where \(g_e \approx 2\) is the electron g-factor, \(-e\) is the electron charge, and \(m_e\) is its mass. This magnetic moment allows the spin state to be controlled using external magnetic fields.

  • Zeeman Splitting: In a static magnetic field \(\mathbf{B}_0 = B_0 \hat{z}\), the interaction Hamiltonian is \(\hat{H}_{Zeeman} = -\mathbf{\mu}_S \cdot \mathbf{B}_0 \approx \frac{g_e e \hbar}{2m_e} B_0 \sigma_z = \hbar\frac{\omega_L}{2} \sigma_z\), where \(\omega_L = \frac{g_e e B_0}{2m_e}\) is the Larmor frequency. This Hamiltonian lifts the degeneracy between the spin states, creating an energy gap \(\Delta E = E_{\uparrow} - E_{\downarrow} = \hbar\omega_L\). The \(|0\rangle\) and \(|1\rangle\) states become energy eigenstates with energies \(\mp \hbar\omega_L / 2\) (relative to the midpoint).

  • Manipulation (ESR): Applying an additional, time-dependent magnetic field \(\mathbf{B}_{AC}(t)\) perpendicular to \(\mathbf{B}_0\) (e.g., oscillating at or near the Larmor frequency \(\omega_L\)) induces transitions between \(|0\rangle\) and \(|1\rangle\). This technique, known as Electron Spin Resonance (ESR), allows for the implementation of arbitrary single-qubit rotations (gates) by controlling the frequency, amplitude, phase, and duration of the applied microwave pulses.

Physical Confinement

To function as a qubit, the electron must be localized and individually addressable. Common methods include:

  • Quantum Dots: Semiconductor nanostructures that confine electrons in all three dimensions, acting like "artificial atoms" with tunable energy levels.

  • Donor Atoms in Silicon: Electrons bound to impurity atoms (like Phosphorus) embedded in a silicon crystal lattice.

  • Nitrogen-Vacancy (NV) Centers in Diamond: Point defects in the diamond lattice consisting of a nitrogen atom adjacent to a vacant site. The electronic spin state of the NV center exhibits long coherence times even at room temperature.

Each platform has advantages and disadvantages regarding scalability, coherence time (how long the quantum state survives before degrading due to environmental interactions), and ease of control and measurement.

Photon Polarization Qubits

Photons, the quanta of the electromagnetic field, are massless bosons with spin \(s=1\). However, due to their massless nature, the projection of their spin along their direction of propagation can only take values \(m_s = \pm 1\), corresponding to left and right circular polarizations. The transverse nature of electromagnetic waves gives rise to two independent polarization states, which can be used to encode a qubit.

Polarization Bases

Several convenient orthonormal bases can be used to describe photon polarization:

  • Linear (Rectilinear) Basis: Horizontal (\(|H\rangle\)) and Vertical (\(|V\rangle\)) polarization. \[|0\rangle \equiv |H\rangle, \quad |1\rangle \equiv |V\rangle.\]

  • Circular Basis: Right (\(|R\rangle\)) and Left (\(|L\rangle\)) circular polarization. These relate to the linear basis as: \[\begin{aligned} |R\rangle &= \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(|H\rangle + i|V\rangle) \label{eq:R_pol} \\ |L\rangle &= \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(|H\rangle - i|V\rangle) \label{eq:L_pol} \end{aligned}\] One might choose \(|0'\rangle \equiv |R\rangle\) and \(|1'\rangle \equiv |L\rangle\).

  • Diagonal Basis: Diagonal (\(|D\rangle\)) and Anti-diagonal (\(|A\rangle\)) polarization, rotated by 45 degrees relative to H/V. \[\begin{aligned} |D\rangle &= \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(|H\rangle + |V\rangle) \label{eq:D_pol} \\ |A\rangle &= \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(|H\rangle - |V\rangle) \label{eq:A_pol} \end{aligned}\]

A general polarization stateA general polarization state is a superposition \(|\psi\rangle = \alpha |H\rangle + \beta |V\rangle\).

Manipulation and Measurement

Photon polarization can be easily manipulated and measured using standard optical components:

  • Polarizers: Project the photon state onto a specific polarization axis, used for state preparation and measurement. For example, a horizontal polarizer transmits \(|H\rangle\) photons and blocks \(|V\rangle\) photons. Measuring in the diagonal basis requires a polarizer oriented at 45 degrees.

  • Wave Plates (Retarders): Birefringent materials that introduce a phase shift between two orthogonal polarization components.

    • Half-Wave Plate (\(\lambda/2\)): Introduces a \(\pi\) phase shift. Rotates linear polarization (e.g., converts \(|H\rangle\) to \(|V\rangle\) if oriented correctly). Implements transformations like the Pauli-X and Pauli-Z gates (depending on orientation and basis).

    • Quarter-Wave Plate (\(\lambda/4\)): Introduces a \(\pi/2\) phase shift. Converts linear to circular polarization and vice versa (e.g., converts \(|H\rangle\) to \(|R\rangle\) if oriented correctly). Implements transformations related to the Hadamard gate.

    By combining wave plates and polarizers, arbitrary single-qubit unitary operations and measurements in different bases can be performed.

Remark. Pros and Cons of Photonic Qubitsrem:photonic_pros_cons Photons are excellent carriers of quantum information over long distances due to their speed and weak interaction with the environment (leading to long coherence times during flight). However, this weak interaction also makes it challenging to implement deterministic two-qubit gates, which are necessary for universal quantum computation. Measurement-based schemes and non-linear optical effects are often employed to overcome this.

Other Qubit Modalities

Besides electron spin and photon polarization, numerous other physical systems are explored for qubit implementation, often leveraging energy quantization in different contexts:

  • Trapped Ions: Individual ions confined by electromagnetic fields. Qubits are encoded in stable electronic energy levels (optical qubits) or hyperfine ground states (hyperfine qubits). Interactions are mediated by shared motional modes (phonons), enabling high-fidelity gates.

  • Superconducting Circuits: Microfabricated circuits containing Josephson junctions, which behave as non-linear inductors. These circuits exhibit quantized energy levels when cooled to cryogenic temperatures. By designing the circuit parameters, one can create anharmonic oscillators (like the "Transmon" qubit mentioned in [rem:qho_qubit_reqs]) where the lowest two levels (\(|0\rangle, |1\rangle\)) can be selectively addressed using microwave pulses, effectively isolating a qubit subspace.

  • Neutral Atoms: Atoms trapped in optical lattices or tweezers. Qubits can be encoded in hyperfine states or Rydberg states (highly excited electronic states).

  • Topological Qubits: Based on exotic quasiparticles (e.g., Majorana fermions) in condensed matter systems, whose non-local properties could offer inherent protection against local perturbations, potentially leading to fault-tolerant quantum computation.

These systems often involve more complex physics and engineering than the simple descriptions of spin or polarization might suggest, but they offer diverse pathways towards scalable quantum computing.

General Considerations: Separating Qubit State from Full System Description

A critical concept, emphasized in the lecture, is that the simple qubit state \(|\psi_{qubit}\rangle = \alpha|0\rangle + \beta|1\rangle\) is an abstraction representing only a specific degree of freedom of a much more complex physical system.

Spatial Degrees of Freedom (Wavefunction)

Any physical particle used as a qubit carrier (electron, atom, photon) also possesses spatial degrees of freedom described by a wavefunction \(\psi(\mathbf{r}, t)\). The complete state of the system resides in a larger Hilbert space, often described by a tensor product: \[|\Psi_{total}(t)\rangle = |\psi_{spatial}(t)\rangle \otimes |\psi_{qubit}(t)\rangle. \label{eq:tensor_product_state}\] For instance, an electron qubit might have a state like \(\Psi(\mathbf{r}, t) = \psi(\mathbf{r}, t) \otimes (\alpha(t)|\downarrow\rangle + \beta(t)|\uparrow\rangle)\). Here, \(\psi(\mathbf{r}, t)\) describes the probability amplitude of finding the electron at position \(\mathbf{r}\) at time \(t\), while the second part describes its spin state.

Remark. Entanglement between Subsystemsrem:subsystem_entanglement In general, the spatial and internal (qubit) degrees of freedom can become entangled, meaning the total state cannot be written as a simple product state like [eq:tensor_product_state]. For example, in the Stern-Gerlach experiment ([box:stern_gerlach]), the interaction entangles the spin state with the spatial trajectory: \(\alpha |\psi_{spatial, \uparrow}\rangle \otimes |\uparrow\rangle + \beta |\psi_{spatial, \downarrow}\rangle \otimes |\downarrow\rangle\), where \(|\psi_{spatial, \uparrow}\rangle\) and \(|\psi_{spatial, \downarrow}\rangle\) represent wavepackets moving along different paths.

Temporal Evolution and Effective Hamiltonians

The time evolution of the total state \(|\Psi_{total}(t)\rangle\) is governed by the full Hamiltonian \(\hat{H}_{total}\) via the Schrödinger equation: \(i\hbar\frac{d}{dt}|\Psi_{total}(t)\rangle = \hat{H}_{total} |\Psi_{total}(t)\rangle\). This Hamiltonian typically includes terms for the spatial dynamics (kinetic and potential energy) and the qubit dynamics (e.g., Zeeman term), as well as any interaction terms: \[\hat{H}_{total} = (\hat{H}_{spatial} \otimes \hat{I}_{qubit}) + (\hat{I}_{spatial} \otimes \hat{H}_{qubit}) + \hat{H}_{interaction}.\] Qubit operations (gates) are implemented by applying control fields, which correspond to adding time-dependent terms to \(\hat{H}_{total}\), ideally designed to act primarily on the qubit subspace (i.e., terms like \(\hat{I}_{spatial} \otimes \hat{H}_{control}(t)\)).

The free evolution under \(\hat{H}_{qubit}\) (e.g., Zeeman splitting) leads to phase accumulation. If \(|0\rangle\) and \(|1\rangle\) are energy eigenstates with energies \(E_0\) and \(E_1\), a state \(\alpha|0\rangle + \beta|1\rangle\) evolves as: \[|\psi(t)\rangle = \alpha e^{-i E_0 t / \hbar} |0\rangle + \beta e^{-i E_1 t / \hbar} |1\rangle = e^{-i E_0 t / \hbar} \left( \alpha |0\rangle + \beta e^{-i (E_1 - E_0) t / \hbar} |1\rangle \right). \label{eq:qubit_time_evolution}\] The overall phase \(e^{-i E_0 t / \hbar}\) is usually unobservable. The physically relevant dynamics are captured by the relative phase \(e^{-i \Delta E t / \hbar}\), where \(\Delta E = E_1 - E_0\).

To simplify analysis and focus on the controllable dynamics relative to the fast free evolution, physicists often employ an effective Hamiltonian or transform into a rotating frame.

  • By mathematically transforming the state vector (e.g., \(|\psi' (t)\rangle = e^{i E_0 t / \hbar} |\psi(t)\rangle\)), the fast oscillation associated with \(E_0\) can be removed.

  • In this rotating frame, the effective Hamiltonian describes the evolution relative to the free precession, making the effect of control fields (which are often applied at or near the transition frequency \(\Delta E / \hbar\)) clearer.

  • The unitary operators \(\hat{U}\) representing quantum gates in quantum computing algorithms typically correspond to the evolution operators generated by these effective Hamiltonians in the rotating frame, acting solely on the two-dimensional qubit subspace \(\text{span}\{|0\rangle, |1\rangle\}\).

Remark. Physical Transmission of Qubitsrem:physical_transmission As stressed in the lecture, when a qubit is physically sent from one location to another (e.g., Alice sending a photon qubit to Bob), it is the entire physical system (the photon) that travels. This system carries both its spatial properties (encoded in its wavefunction, determining its path and timing) and its internal qubit state (encoded in its polarization). While our logical description focuses on the \(\alpha|0\rangle + \beta|1\rangle\) part, the physical reality involves the propagation of the complete entity \(|\psi_{spatial}\rangle \otimes |\psi_{qubit}\rangle\). Understanding this distinction is crucial when considering physical implementation, communication protocols, and sources of error.

Review of Qubit Principles

Having explored specific physical systems, let’s consolidate the fundamental mathematical and conceptual framework used to describe qubits, independent of their particular physical realization. These principles form the core postulates of quantum mechanics as applied to two-level systems.

State Representation and Superposition

Qubit State Spacedefn:qubit_state_space A qubit is a quantum system whose state is described by a vector in a two-dimensional complex Hilbert space, denoted \(\mathcal{H}\). This space is isomorphic to \(\mathbb{C}^2\).

We typically work with a preferred orthonormal basis for this space, called the computational basis, denoted by \(\{|0\rangle, |1\rangle\}\). These basis vectors correspond to the two distinguishable classical states or measurement outcomes (e.g., spin down/up, horizontal/vertical polarization, ground/excited energy state). In matrix representation: \[|0\rangle \equiv \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}, \quad |1\rangle \equiv \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}.\] The defining characteristic of quantum mechanics is the principle of superposition. A general state of a qubit, \(|\psi\rangle\), can be any linear combination of the basis states: \[|\psi\rangle = \alpha |0\rangle + \beta |1\rangle = \begin{pmatrix} \alpha \\ \beta \end{pmatrix}, \label{eq:qubit_superposition}\] where \(\alpha, \beta \in \mathbb{C}\) are complex numbers called probability amplitudes. The state \(|\psi\rangle\) represents a coherent superposition, meaning the qubit exists simultaneously in relation to both basis states, weighted by the amplitudes \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\).

Normalization Conditionprop:normalization This proposition states that the total probability of finding the qubit in any state must be 1, which translates to a condition on the state vector’s length (norm).

The state vector \(|\psi\rangle\) must be normalized, meaning its norm squared is equal to 1: \[\left\||\psi\rangle\right\|^2 = \langle\psi|\psi\rangle = (\langle 0|\alpha^* + \langle 1|\beta^*) (\alpha |0\rangle + \beta |1\rangle) = \alpha^*\alpha + \beta^*\beta = \left|\alpha\right|^2 + \left|\beta\right|^2 = 1. \label{eq:normalization}\] This condition ensures that the probabilities of all possible measurement outcomes sum to unity, as required by probability theory.

While \(|\psi\rangle\) lives in \(\mathbb{C}^2\) (four real parameters), the normalization condition ([eq:normalization]) and the fact that the overall phase of a quantum state is unobservable reduce the number of independent real parameters needed to specify a pure single-qubit state to two. We can write the state (up to a global phase) as: \[|\psi\rangle = \cos\left(\frac{\theta}{2}\right) |0\rangle + e^{i\phi} \sin\left(\frac{\theta}{2}\right) |1\rangle, \label{eq:bloch_sphere_param}\] where \(\theta \in [0, \pi]\) and \(\phi \in [0, 2\pi)\) are real parameters. These angles define a point on the surface of a unit sphere in \(\mathbb{R}^3\), known as the Bloch sphere.

  • The north pole (\(\theta=0\)) corresponds to \(|0\rangle\).

  • The south pole (\(\theta=\pi\)) corresponds to \(|1\rangle\).

  • Points on the equator (\(\theta=\pi/2\)) represent equal superpositions of \(|0\rangle\) and \(|1\rangle\), such as \(|+\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(|0\rangle+|1\rangle)\) (at \(\phi=0\)) and \(|i\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(|0\rangle+i|1\rangle)\) (at \(\phi=\pi/2\)).

  • Orthogonal states correspond to antipodal points on the sphere.

The Bloch sphere provides a useful visualization tool for single-qubit states and the effect of single-qubit gates (which correspond to rotations of the state vector on the sphere).

Measurement Postulate and Probabilities

Quantum measurement is fundamentally probabilistic and invasive, meaning it generally disturbs the state being measured. The standard measurement postulate describes projective measurements in a chosen basis.

Born Rule for Qubitsprop:born_rule This proposition, known as the Born rule, provides the fundamental link between the mathematical description of a quantum state (the amplitudes) and the probabilities of observing specific outcomes upon measurement. It also describes the state collapse after measurement.

Consider a qubit in the state \(|\psi\rangle = \alpha |0\rangle + \beta |1\rangle\). If a measurement is performed in the computational basis \(\{|0\rangle, |1\rangle\}\), the following rules apply:

  • The measurement outcome will be either 0 or 1.

  • The probability of obtaining outcome 0 is \(P(0) = \left|\alpha\right|^2\). If outcome 0 is obtained, the state of the qubit immediately after the measurement collapses to \(|0\rangle\).

  • The probability of obtaining outcome 1 is \(P(1) = \left|\beta\right|^2\). If outcome 1 is obtained, the state of the qubit immediately after the measurement collapses to \(|1\rangle\).

The probabilities sum to one, \(P(0) + P(1) = \left|\alpha\right|^2 + \left|\beta\right|^2 = 1\), consistent with the normalization condition ([eq:normalization]).

Remark. Measurement in Different Basesrem:measurement_bases Measurements can be performed in any orthonormal basis. For example, measuring in the diagonal basis \(\{|+\rangle, |-\rangle\}\) (where \(|\pm\rangle = (|0\rangle \pm |1\rangle)/\sqrt{2}\)) corresponds physically to measuring the Pauli-X operator \(\sigma_x\). To find the probabilities, one first expresses the state \(|\psi\rangle\) in the measurement basis: \(|\psi\rangle = \alpha' |+\rangle + \beta' |-\rangle\). The probabilities are then \(P(+) = \left|\alpha'\right|^2\) and \(P(-) = \left|\beta'\right|^2\). Physically, this might involve rotating the basis before measurement (e.g., using a Hadamard gate or rotating a polarizer).

Remark. Connection to Physical Systemsrem:measurement_physical

  • Stern-Gerlach: Measuring the spin projection \(S_z\) corresponds to a measurement in the \(\{|\downarrow\rangle, |\uparrow\rangle\}\) basis. The probabilities of deflection up or down are given by the squared magnitudes of the amplitudes for \(|\uparrow\rangle\) and \(|\downarrow\rangle\) in the initial state.

  • Photon Polarization: Passing a photon through a vertical polarizer performs a measurement in the \(\{|H\rangle, |V\rangle\}\) basis. The probability of transmission (outcome V) is \(\left|\beta\right|^2\) if the state is \(\alpha|H\rangle + \beta|V\rangle\), and the transmitted photon is in the state \(|V\rangle\).

Evolution via Unitary Operators

The third fundamental postulate governs how quantum states evolve in time when the system is not being measured.

Unitary Evolutionprop:unitary_evolution This proposition states that the time evolution of a closed quantum system (one not interacting with its environment or being measured) is described by a unitary transformation, which preserves the quantum state’s normalization and is reversible.

The evolution of a closed quantum system is described by a unitary transformation. If the state of the system at time \(t_1\) is \(|\psi(t_1)\rangle\), its state at a later time \(t_2\) is given by: \[|\psi(t_2)\rangle = \hat{U}(t_2, t_1) |\psi(t_1)\rangle, \label{eq:unitary_evolution}\] where \(\hat{U}(t_2, t_1)\) is a unitary operator mapping the Hilbert space onto itself. A linear operator \(\hat{U}\) is unitary if its adjoint (conjugate transpose) \(\hat{U}^\dagger\) is also its inverse: \[\hat{U}^\dagger \hat{U} = \hat{U} \hat{U}^\dagger = \hat{I}, \label{eq:unitarity_condition}\] where \(\hat{I}\) is the identity operator.

Remark. Properties of Unitary Evolutionrem:unitary_props

  • Preservation of Norm: Unitary operators preserve the inner product between states, and consequently, the norm of state vectors. If \(|\phi\rangle = \hat{U}|\psi\rangle\), then \(\langle\phi|\phi\rangle = \langle\psi|\hat{U}^\dagger\hat{U}|\psi\rangle = \langle\psi|\psi\rangle\). This ensures that a normalized state remains normalized throughout its evolution, consistent with the conservation of probability.

  • Reversibility: Since \(\hat{U}\) has an inverse (\(\hat{U}^\dagger\)), quantum evolution is reversible. Knowing the state at time \(t_2\), one can determine the state at \(t_1\) by applying \(\hat{U}^\dagger(t_2, t_1) = \hat{U}(t_1, t_2)\).

  • Connection to Schrödinger Equation: For a system described by a time-independent Hamiltonian \(\hat{H}\), the evolution operator is given by: \[\hat{U}(t_2, t_1) = e^{-i\hat{H}(t_2-t_1)/\hbar}.\label{eq:evolution_operator_hamiltonian}\] The operator \(\hat{H}\) is Hermitian (\(\hat{H}^\dagger = \hat{H}\)), which ensures that \(\hat{U}\) defined this way is unitary. The state evolution \(|\psi(t)\rangle = e^{-i\hat{H}t/\hbar}|\psi(0)\rangle\) is the solution to the time-dependent Schrödinger equation \(i\hbar\frac{d}{dt}|\psi(t)\rangle = \hat{H} |\psi(t)\rangle\).

Remark. Quantum Gates as Unitary Operatorsrem:gates_unitary In quantum computation, logical operations on qubits are represented by specific unitary operators called quantum gates. Examples include the Pauli gates (\(\hat{X}, \hat{Y}, \hat{Z}\)), the Hadamard gate (\(\hat{H}\)), phase gates (\(\hat{S}, \hat{T}\)), and multi-qubit gates like CNOT. These gates correspond to physically applying carefully controlled interactions (e.g., timed laser or microwave pulses, as discussed in [subsec:electron_spin,subsec:photon_polarization]) that implement the desired Hamiltonian evolution over a specific duration, resulting in the target unitary transformation on the qubit state(s).

Remark. Implementing Unitary Operatorsrem:implementing_unitary The lecture mentioned that "it’s very easy to build" unitary operators. This likely refers to the theoretical principle that any unitary transformation can, in principle, be generated by some Hamiltonian evolution according to [eq:evolution_operator_hamiltonian]. Finding the specific physical interactions (i.e., the Hamiltonian \(\hat{H}\)) and controlling them precisely over time to implement a desired \(\hat{U}\) with high fidelity is the central challenge of experimental quantum computing. While the existence of such Hamiltonians is guaranteed, their practical realization requires sophisticated experimental techniques and careful engineering to isolate the system and apply the correct control fields.

Conclusion

This lecture has navigated key concepts from quantum mechanics that form the physical basis for quantum information processing and the realization of qubits. The journey aimed to connect foundational quantum principles with their manifestation in tangible systems.

We began with the Quantum Harmonic Oscillator (2), a ubiquitous model demonstrating energy quantization (\(E_n = (n+1/2)\hbar\omega_0\)) and the characteristic equispaced energy levels. This system illustrates how discrete energy states in confined systems can serve as a basis for encoding quantum information, often utilizing the lowest two levels (\(|n=0\rangle, |n=1\rangle\)) and leveraging anharmonicity in real systems for addressability.

Next, we explored Angular Momentum in quantum mechanics (3), emphasizing its quantization rules. We distinguished between orbital angular momentum (\(\hat{L}\)) and the intrinsic, non-classical property of spin (\(\hat{S}\)). The quantization of spin projection, particularly for spin-1/2 particles like electrons (\(m_s = \pm 1/2\)), provides a natural and widely used two-level system for qubit implementation.

The Stern-Gerlach experiment ([box:stern_gerlach]) was presented as pivotal historical evidence confirming the quantization of angular momentum projection (spatial quantization) and providing strong support for the existence of electron spin. Its outcome—discrete deflection rather than a continuous smear—starkly contrasts classical expectations.

We then surveyed specific Physical Systems for Qubit Implementation (4), focusing on electron spin qubits (manipulated via magnetic fields in confined environments like quantum dots or NV centers) and photon polarization qubits (manipulated using optical elements like wave plates and polarizers). Other modalities like trapped ions and superconducting circuits were also briefly mentioned, highlighting the diversity of platforms being developed.

A crucial point emphasized was the necessary abstraction involved in the qubit model (4.4). The simple state \(\alpha|0\rangle + \beta|1\rangle\) represents only a specific degree of freedom within a more complex physical entity that includes spatial wavefunctions and overall temporal dynamics (\(|\Psi_{total}\rangle = |\psi_{spatial}\rangle \otimes |\psi_{qubit}\rangle\)). Understanding this separation, often managed via effective Hamiltonians and rotating frames ([box:rotating_frame]), is vital for appreciating both the power and the challenges of quantum control.

Finally, we reviewed the core Qubit Principles (5): state representation in a Hilbert space ([defn:qubit_state_space]), the principle of superposition allowing simultaneous existence in multiple basis states, the probabilistic nature of measurement governed by the Born rule ([prop:born_rule]) and associated state collapse, and the deterministic evolution of closed systems via unitary transformations ([prop:unitary_evolution]) (quantum gates), fundamentally linked to the Schrödinger equation.

Key Takeaways

  • Quantization is Key: The discreteness of physical properties (energy levels in confined systems, angular momentum projections) is a fundamental quantum effect that provides the distinct states (\(|0\rangle, |1\rangle\)) needed to define a qubit.

  • Intrinsic Properties Offer Resources: Nature provides intrinsic quantum properties like electron spin, which inherently form two-level systems well-suited for qubit encoding.

  • Abstraction is Powerful but Incomplete: The abstract qubit formalism (\(\mathbb{C}^2\) Hilbert space, unitary gates) is essential for developing algorithms and understanding information processing, but realizing these operations requires careful management of the complex underlying physics of the host system.

# The Quantum Harmonic Oscillator {#sec:qho}

The Quantum Harmonic Oscillator (QHO) serves as a fundamental and
exactly solvable model in quantum mechanics. It describes a system
experiencing a restoring force proportional to its displacement from a
stable equilibrium position, analogous to a mass attached to an ideal
spring obeying Hooke's law. Its importance extends far beyond this
simple picture, providing a basis for understanding phenomena ranging
from molecular vibrations to the quantization of fields.

## Classical Analogue and Significance {#subsec:qho_classical}

Classically, the harmonic oscillator is characterized by a potential
energy function that is quadratic in the displacement $x$ from
equilibrium:
$$V(x) = \frac{1}{2} k x^2 = \frac{1}{2} m \omega_0^2 x^2.    \label{eq:classical_potential}$$
Here, $k$ represents the spring constant, $m$ is the mass of the
oscillating particle, and $\omega_0 = \sqrt{k/m}$ is the natural angular
frequency of oscillation. The force exerted on the particle is
$F(x) = -\frac{dV}{dx} = -kx$, always directed towards the equilibrium
point $x=0$.

::: remark
Reduced Massrem:reduced_mass In systems involving two interacting bodies
(e.g., a diatomic molecule), the analysis of relative motion often leads
to an equivalent one-body problem. In such cases, the mass $m$ in
[\[eq:classical_potential\]](#eq:classical_potential){reference-type="ref+label"
reference="eq:classical_potential"} is replaced by the reduced mass
$\mu = \frac{m_1 m_2}{m_1 + m_2}$, where $m_1$ and $m_2$ are the masses
of the two bodies.
:::

::: tcolorbox
The harmonic oscillator model is remarkably pervasive in physics for
several reasons:

-   **Approximation near Equilibrium:** As highlighted in the lecture,
    any physical system near a point of stable equilibrium (a minimum of
    its potential energy $V$) behaves approximately like a harmonic
    oscillator for small displacements. A Taylor expansion of $V(x)$
    around the minimum $x_0$ yields:
    $$V(x) \approx V(x_0) + \underbrace{V'(x_0)}_{=0 \text{ at min}} (x-x_0) + \frac{1}{2} \underbrace{V''(x_0)}_{=k > 0} (x-x_0)^2 + \mathcal{O}((x-x_0)^3).            \label{eq:taylor_expansion}$$
    Ignoring the constant energy offset $V(x_0)$ and higher-order terms,
    the potential is quadratic, corresponding to harmonic oscillation
    with an effective spring constant $k = V''(x_0)$.

-   **Fundamental Excitations:** The QHO framework is essential for
    describing fundamental excitations in various physical systems,
    including:

    -   Vibrations of atoms in molecules.

    -   Lattice vibrations in crystalline solids (phonons).

    -   Quantization of the electromagnetic field (photons), where each
        mode of the field behaves as an independent QHO.
:::

## Quantum Hamiltonian and Energy Quantization {#subsec:qho_quantum}

To transition to quantum mechanics, we promote the classical position
$x$ and momentum $p$ to operators $\hat{x}$ and $\hat{p}$, respectively.
In the position representation, $\hat{x} = x$ and
$\hat{p} = -i\hbar\frac{d}{dx}$. These operators satisfy the canonical
commutation relation $[\hat{x}, \hat{p}] = i\hbar$. The Hamiltonian
operator for the one-dimensional QHO is obtained by substituting these
operators into the classical Hamiltonian function $H = p^2/(2m) + V(x)$:
$$\hat{H} = \frac{\hat{p}^2}{2m} + \frac{1}{2} m \omega_0^2 \hat{x}^2 = -\frac{\hbar^2}{2m} \frac{d^2}{dx^2} + \frac{1}{2} m \omega_0^2 x^2.    \label{eq:qho_hamiltonian}$$
The energy eigenstates $|\psi_n\rangle$ and corresponding energy
eigenvalues $E_n$ are found by solving the time-independent Schrödinger
equation: $$\hat{H} |\psi_n\rangle = E_n |\psi_n\rangle.$$ The solutions
reveal that the energy spectrum is discrete (quantized).

::: theorem
Energy Eigenvalues of the QHOthm:qho_energy This theorem specifies the
discrete energy levels allowed for a quantum harmonic oscillator.

The allowed energy levels for the one-dimensional quantum harmonic
oscillator are given by:
$$E_n = \left(n + \frac{1}{2}\right) \hbar\omega_0 = \left(n + \frac{1}{2}\right) h \nu_0, \quad \text{for } n = 0, 1, 2, \dots        \label{eq:qho_energy}$$
where $\nu_0 = \omega_0 / (2\pi)$ is the natural frequency,
$h = 2\pi \hbar$ is Planck's constant, and $n$ is the energy level
quantum number.
:::

::: remark
Properties of QHO Energy Levelsrem:qho_levels

-   **Quantization:** Only discrete energy values $E_n$ are permitted,
    in contrast to the continuous range of energies allowed classically.

-   **Equispaced Levels:** The energy levels are uniformly spaced, with
    a constant energy difference between adjacent levels:
    $$\Delta E = E_{n+1} - E_n = \hbar\omega_0 = h \nu_0.            \label{eq:energy_spacing}$$
    This means that transitions between adjacent levels always involve
    the absorption or emission of the same quantum of energy,
    $\hbar\omega_0$.

-   **Zero-Point Energy:** The lowest possible energy state (ground
    state), corresponding to $n=0$, has a non-zero energy
    $E_0 = \frac{1}{2} \hbar\omega_0$. This \"zero-point energy\" is a
    fundamental consequence of the Heisenberg uncertainty principle; the
    particle cannot be perfectly localized at the potential minimum with
    zero momentum simultaneously.
:::

## Ladder Operator Formalism {#subsec:qho_ladder}

An elegant and powerful method for analyzing the QHO involves the
introduction of non-Hermitian ladder operators, the annihilation
operator $\hat{a}$ and the creation operator $\hat{a}^\dagger$:
$$\begin{aligned}    \hat{a} &= \sqrt{\frac{m\omega_0}{2\hbar}} \left( \hat{x} + \frac{i}{m\omega_0} \hat{p} \right) \label{eq:annihilation_op} \\    \hat{a}^\dagger &= \sqrt{\frac{m\omega_0}{2\hbar}} \left( \hat{x} - \frac{i}{m\omega_0} \hat{p} \right) \label{eq:creation_op}\end{aligned}$$
These operators satisfy the fundamental commutation relation:
$$[\hat{a}, \hat{a}^\dagger] = \hat{a}\hat{a}^\dagger - \hat{a}^\dagger\hat{a} = 1.    \label{eq:ladder_commutation}$$
In terms of these operators, the position and momentum operators can be
expressed as:
$$\begin{aligned}    \hat{x} &= \sqrt{\frac{\hbar}{2m\omega_0}} (\hat{a} + \hat{a}^\dagger) \\    \hat{p} &= i \sqrt{\frac{m\omega_0\hbar}{2}} (\hat{a}^\dagger - \hat{a})\end{aligned}$$
Crucially, the Hamiltonian
([\[eq:qho_hamiltonian\]](#eq:qho_hamiltonian){reference-type="ref+label"
reference="eq:qho_hamiltonian"}) takes a very simple form:
$$\hat{H} = \hbar\omega_0 \left( \hat{a}^\dagger \hat{a} + \frac{1}{2} \right).    \label{eq:hamiltonian_ladder}$$
The operator $\hat{N} = \hat{a}^\dagger \hat{a}$ is called the number
operator. Its eigenvalues are the non-negative integers
$n=0, 1, 2, \dots$, confirming the energy spectrum
$E_n = \hbar\omega_0 (n + 1/2)$. The eigenstates $|n\rangle$ are
simultaneous eigenstates of $\hat{H}$ and $\hat{N}$.

The names \"annihilation\" and \"creation\" stem from their effect on
the energy eigenstates $|n\rangle$:
$$\begin{aligned}    \hat{a} |n\rangle &= \sqrt{n} |n-1\rangle \quad (\text{for } n \ge 1), \quad \hat{a} |0\rangle = 0 \\    \hat{a}^\dagger |n\rangle &= \sqrt{n+1} |n+1\rangle\end{aligned}$$
The annihilation operator $\hat{a}$ lowers the energy level by one
quantum $\hbar\omega_0$ (destroying one energy quantum), while the
creation operator $\hat{a}^\dagger$ raises the energy level by one
quantum (creating one energy quantum). This formalism directly
demonstrates the equispaced nature of the energy levels and provides a
simple way to construct all excited states starting from the ground
state $|0\rangle$ (defined by $\hat{a}|0\rangle=0$) via repeated
application of $\hat{a}^\dagger$.

## Potential for Qubit Encoding {#subsec:qho_qubit}

The discrete and equally spaced energy levels of the QHO provide a
natural ladder for encoding information. Specifically, the two lowest
energy states can be chosen to represent the computational basis states
of a qubit.

::: example
Qubit Encoding in QHOex:qho_qubit A common encoding scheme identifies:
$$\begin{aligned}        |0\rangle_{\text{qubit}} &\equiv |n=0\rangle \quad (\text{Ground state, Energy } E_0 = \frac{1}{2} \hbar\omega_0) \\        |1\rangle_{\text{qubit}} &\equiv |n=1\rangle \quad (\text{First excited state, Energy } E_1 = \frac{3}{2} \hbar\omega_0)    \end{aligned}$$
A general qubit state
$|\psi\rangle = \alpha|0\rangle_{\text{qubit}} + \beta|1\rangle_{\text{qubit}}$
then corresponds to a quantum superposition of the oscillator being in
its ground and first excited states. Transitions between $|0\rangle$
andA general qubit state
$|\psi\rangle = \alpha|0\rangle_{\text{qubit}} + \beta|1\rangle_{\text{qubit}}$
then corresponds to a quantum superposition of the oscillator being in
its ground and first excited states. Transitions between $|0\rangle$ and
$|1\rangle$ can be driven by applying an external field (e.g.,
electromagnetic radiation) resonant with the energy difference
$\Delta E = E_1 - E_0 = \hbar\omega_0$.
:::

::: remark
Requirements and Considerations for Qubit Encodingrem:qho_qubit_reqs

-   **Addressability:** To function as a qubit, one must be able to
    selectively induce transitions between the chosen $|0\rangle$ and
    $|1\rangle$ states without significantly populating higher energy
    states ($|n=2\rangle, |n=3\rangle, \dots$).

-   **Anharmonicity:** Real physical systems that approximate harmonic
    oscillators (e.g., molecular bonds, superconducting circuits) often
    exhibit \*anharmonicity\*. This means the potential $V(x)$ deviates
    from the perfect quadratic form at larger displacements, causing the
    energy level spacing to become non-uniform. For instance,
    $E_2 - E_1$ might differ slightly from $E_1 - E_0$. While this
    deviates from the ideal QHO model, anharmonicity can be advantageous
    for qubit encoding. A slightly smaller gap between $E_1$ and $E_2$
    than between $E_0$ and $E_1$ allows driving frequencies to be tuned
    to specifically address the $0 \leftrightarrow 1$ transition without
    exciting the $1 \leftrightarrow 2$ transition, improving qubit
    control fidelity.

-   **Temperature:** Thermal energy ($k_B T$) must typically be kept
    much smaller than the energy gap $\hbar\omega_0$
    ($k_B T \ll \hbar\omega_0$) to prevent unwanted thermal excitations
    out of the qubit subspace. This often necessitates operation at
    cryogenic temperatures.

-   **General Principle:** The use of energy levels for qubit encoding
    is a general feature of confined quantum systems. Any system
    exhibiting discrete energy levels due to confinement (e.g.,
    electrons in atoms, particles in quantum wells) can potentially host
    qubits, provided suitable levels can be isolated and controlled.
:::

# Angular Momentum in Quantum Mechanics {#sec:angular_momentum}

Angular momentum, a measure of rotational motion, is a fundamental
conserved quantity in classical physics. In quantum mechanics, it
retains its importance but exhibits distinct, quantized properties that
are crucial for understanding atomic structure and provide key resources
for quantum information processing, particularly through the concept of
spin.

## Classical Definition and Quantum Operators {#subsec:am_operators}

Classically, the orbital angular momentum $\mathbf{L}$ of a particle
with position vector $\mathbf{r}$ and linear momentum $\mathbf{p}$ is
defined by the vector product:
$$\mathbf{L} = \mathbf{r} \times \mathbf{p}.$$ The Cartesian components
are given by:
$$\begin{aligned}    L_x &= y p_z - z p_y, \\    L_y &= z p_x - x p_z, \\    L_z &= x p_y - y p_x.\end{aligned}$$
Following the standard quantization procedure, we replace the classical
position and momentum components with their corresponding quantum
operators: $\hat{x}, \hat{y}, \hat{z}$ and
$\hat{p}_x = -i\hbar\frac{\partial}{\partial x}$,
$\hat{p}_y = -i\hbar\frac{\partial}{\partial y}$,
$\hat{p}_z = -i\hbar\frac{\partial}{\partial z}$. This yields the
quantum mechanical orbital angular momentum operators:
$$\begin{aligned}    \hat{L}_x &= \hat{y} \hat{p}_z - \hat{z} \hat{p}_y = -i\hbar\left( y \frac{\partial}{\partial z} - z \frac{\partial}{\partial y} \right), \label{eq:Lx_op} \\    \hat{L}_y &= \hat{z} \hat{p}_x - \hat{x} \hat{p}_z = -i\hbar\left( z \frac{\partial}{\partial x} - x \frac{\partial}{\partial z} \right), \label{eq:Ly_op} \\    \hat{L}_z &= \hat{x} \hat{p}_y - \hat{y} \hat{p}_x = -i\hbar\left( x \frac{\partial}{\partial y} - y \frac{\partial}{\partial x} \right). \label{eq:Lz_op}\end{aligned}$$
A fundamental property of these operators is their non-commutativity.
They obey the cyclic commutation relations:
$$[\hat{L}_x, \hat{L}_y] = i\hbar\hat{L}_z, \quad [\hat{L}_y, \hat{L}_z] = i\hbar\hat{L}_x, \quad [\hat{L}_z, \hat{L}_x] = i\hbar\hat{L}_y.    \label{eq:L_commutation}$$
These can be summarized compactly using the Levi-Civita symbol
$\epsilon_{ijk}$:
$[\hat{L}_i, \hat{L}_j] = i\hbar\sum_{k} \epsilon_{ijk} \hat{L}_k$. The
non-commutativity implies, via the generalized uncertainty principle,
that it is impossible to simultaneously determine the precise values of
more than one component of the angular momentum vector.

However, the square of the total angular momentum operator, defined as
$$\hat{L}^2 = \hat{L}_x^2 + \hat{L}_y^2 + \hat{L}_z^2,$$ commutes with
each individual component:
$$[\hat{L}^2, \hat{L}_i] = 0 \quad \text{for } i = x, y, z.    \label{eq:L2_commutation}$$
This crucial property allows us to find simultaneous eigenstates of
$\hat{L}^2$ and *one* component, conventionally chosen as $\hat{L}_z$.
These eigenstates form the basis for describing the angular momentum
state of a quantum system.

## Quantization of Angular Momentum Eigenvalues {#subsec:am_quantization}

The algebraic structure defined by the commutation relations
[\[eq:L_commutation,eq:L2_commutation\]](#eq:L_commutation,eq:L2_commutation){reference-type="ref+label"
reference="eq:L_commutation,eq:L2_commutation"} dictates the possible
eigenvalues of $\hat{L}^2$ and $\hat{L}_z$.

::: theorem
Angular Momentum Eigenvaluesthm:am_eigenvalues This theorem establishes
the quantized values that the square of the total angular momentum
($\hat{L}^2$) and one of its components (conventionally $\hat{L}_z$) can
take in quantum mechanics, based on the commutation relations of the
angular momentum operators.

The simultaneous eigenstates of $\hat{L}^2$ and $\hat{L}_z$, denoted by
$|l, m\rangle$, satisfy the eigenvalue equations:
$$\begin{aligned}        \hat{L}^2 |l, m\rangle &= \hbar^2 l(l+1) |l, m\rangle, \label{eq:L2_eigenvalue} \\        \hat{L}_z |l, m\rangle &= \hbar m |l, m\rangle. \label{eq:Lz_eigenvalue}    \end{aligned}$$
The quantum numbers $l$ and $m$ are restricted to the following values:

-   $l$ is the **azimuthal** or **orbital angular momentum quantum
    number**. For orbital angular momentum derived from
    $\mathbf{r} \times \mathbf{p}$, $l$ must be a non-negative integer:
    $l = 0, 1, 2, \dots$.

-   $m$ is the **magnetic quantum number**. For a given value of $l$,
    $m$ can take on $(2l+1)$ integer values, ranging from $-l$ to $+l$
    in integer steps: $m = -l, -l+1, \dots, l-1, l$.

The states $|l, m\rangle$ for fixed $l$ and varying $m$ form an
orthonormal basis for the subspace associated with angular momentum $l$.
:::

::: remark
Magnitude vs. Projection and the Vector Modelrem:am_vector_model

-   The eigenvalue of $\hat{L}^2$ determines the square of the magnitude
    of the angular momentum vector. The magnitude itself is
    $\left\|\mathbf{L}\right\| = \sqrt{\hbar^2 l(l+1)} = \hbar\sqrt{l(l+1)}$.

-   The eigenvalue of $\hat{L}_z$ gives the projection of the angular
    momentum vector onto the z-axis, which is $\hbar m$.

-   The maximum possible projection along the z-axis is $\hbar l$. Since
    $\sqrt{l(l+1)} > l$ for $l > 0$, the magnitude of the angular
    momentum vector is always strictly greater than its maximum
    projection onto any single axis.

-   This leads to the \"vector model\" visualization: the angular
    momentum vector $\mathbf{L}$ can be thought of as precessing around
    the z-axis, maintaining a constant magnitude $\hbar\sqrt{l(l+1)}$
    and a constant z-projection $\hbar m$, but with indeterminate $L_x$
    and $L_y$ components. The vector lies on the surface of a cone with
    height $\hbar m$ and slant height $\hbar\sqrt{l(l+1)}$.
:::

::: remark
Spherical Harmonicsrem:spherical_harmonics When working in the position
representation (using spherical coordinates $r, \theta, \phi$), the
simultaneous eigenstates $\langle\theta, \phi|l, m\rangle$ are the
spherical harmonics $Y_{lm}(\theta, \phi)$. These functions describe the
angular dependence of wavefunctions for systems with definite angular
momentum, such as electron orbitals in atoms.
:::

## Spin as Intrinsic Angular Momentum {#subsec:spin}

Experiments revealed that elementary particles like electrons possess an
additional form of angular momentum that is *intrinsic*, not associated
with their spatial motion ($\mathbf{r} \times \mathbf{p}$). This
intrinsic angular momentum is called **spin**, denoted by
$\hat{\mathbf{S}}$.

::: definition
Spin Angular Momentumdefn:spin Spin
$\hat{\mathbf{S}} = (\hat{S}_x, \hat{S}_y, \hat{S}_z)$ is an intrinsic
angular momentum vector operator whose components obey the same
commutation relations as orbital angular momentum:
$$[\hat{S}_i, \hat{S}_j] = i\hbar\sum_{k} \epsilon_{ijk} \hat{S}_k.$$
Similarly, the square of the spin operator
$\hat{S}^2 = \hat{S}_x^2 + \hat{S}_y^2 + \hat{S}_z^2$ commutes with its
components: $[\hat{S}^2, \hat{S}_i] = 0$. The simultaneous eigenstates
of $\hat{S}^2$ and $\hat{S}_z$, denoted $|s, m_s\rangle$, satisfy:
$$\begin{aligned}        \hat{S}^2 |s, m_s\rangle &= \hbar^2 s(s+1) |s, m_s\rangle, \\        \hat{S}_z |s, m_s\rangle &= \hbar m_s |s, m_s\rangle.    \end{aligned}$$
Here, $s$ is the **spin quantum number**, which is a fixed
characteristic of a given type of particle. Unlike the orbital quantum
number $l$, $s$ can be a non-negative integer *or* a half-integer
($s = 0, 1/2, 1, 3/2, \dots$). For a given $s$, the spin magnetic
quantum number $m_s$ takes on $(2s+1)$ values:
$m_s = -s, -s+1, \dots, s-1, s$.
:::

::: example
Electron Spin (Spin-1/2)ex:electron_spin Electrons, protons, and
neutrons are fundamental examples of spin-1/2 particles ($s=1/2$).

-   The eigenvalue of $\hat{S}^2$ is fixed:
    $\hbar^2 s(s+1) = \hbar^2 (\frac{1}{2})(\frac{1}{2}+1) = \frac{3}{4} \hbar^2$.

-   The spin magnetic quantum number $m_s$ can only take two values:
    $m_s = -1/2$ and $m_s = +1/2$.

-   The corresponding eigenvalues of $\hat{S}_z$ are $-\frac{\hbar}{2}$
    and $+\frac{\hbar}{2}$.

-   These two basis states represent the intrinsic spin degree of
    freedom of the electron. They are commonly denoted as:

    -   $|\downarrow\rangle$ or $|s=1/2, m_s=-1/2\rangle$: \"spin down\"
        state.

    -   $|\uparrow\rangle$ or $|s=1/2, m_s=+1/2\rangle$: \"spin up\"
        state.

-   This naturally forms a two-dimensional Hilbert space, making
    spin-1/2 systems ideal candidates for implementing qubits, where
    $|0\rangle \equiv |\downarrow\rangle$ and
    $|1\rangle \equiv |\uparrow\rangle$ (or vice versa).
:::

::: remark
Spin Measurement Outcomes: $\pm 1$ vs
$\pm \hbar/2$rem:spin_measurement_units The lecture mentions measuring
spin projection and obtaining outcomes $+1$ or $-1$. This common
convention arises from measuring the spin projection in units of
$\hbar/2$. That is, the observable being measured is effectively
$\frac{2}{\hbar} \hat{S}_z$, whose eigenvalues are
$2 m_s = 2(\pm 1/2) = \pm 1$. The fundamental eigenvalues of $\hat{S}_z$
itself remain $\pm \hbar/2$.
:::

::: remark
Pauli Matricesrem:pauli_matrices For spin-1/2 systems, the spin
operators $\hat{S}_x, \hat{S}_y, \hat{S}_z$ acting on the
two-dimensional spin space $\{|\uparrow\rangle, |\downarrow\rangle\}$
can be represented by matrices proportional to the Pauli matrices
$\sigma_x, \sigma_y, \sigma_z$:
$$\hat{S}_i = \frac{\hbar}{2} \sigma_i,$$ where
$$\sigma_x = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}, \quad        \sigma_y = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -i \\ i & 0 \end{pmatrix}, \quad        \sigma_z = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & -1 \end{pmatrix}.$$
These matrices are fundamental in the description and manipulation of
qubits.
:::

## The Stern-Gerlach Experiment: Evidence for Spin Quantization {#subsec:stern_gerlach}

The Stern-Gerlach experiment, performed by Otto Stern and Walther
Gerlach in 1922, provided the first direct experimental evidence for the
quantization of angular momentum projection, later understood to be
dominated by electron spin.

:::: tcolorbox
**Setup:**

-   A beam of neutral atoms (originally Silver, Ag) is produced in an
    oven and collimated. Silver atoms have a single valence electron in
    an s-orbital ($l=0$), so their total angular momentum is dominated
    by the spin of this electron ($s=1/2$).

-   The beam passes through a region with a strong *inhomogeneous*
    magnetic field, typically designed to have a significant gradient in
    one direction (e.g., $\partial B_z / \partial z \neq 0$).

-   A detection screen is placed after the magnet to observe the spatial
    distribution of the atoms.

**Physics:**

-   An atom with magnetic dipole moment $\mathbf{\mu}$ in a magnetic
    field $\mathbf{B}$ experiences a potential energy
    $U = -\mathbf{\mu} \cdot \mathbf{B}$.

-   In an inhomogeneous field, this leads to a net force
    $\mathbf{F} = -\nabla U = \nabla (\mathbf{\mu} \cdot \mathbf{B})$.

-   If the field is primarily $B_z(z)$ and the gradient is
    $\partial B_z / \partial z$, the dominant force is in the
    z-direction: $F_z \approx \mu_z \frac{\partial B_z}{\partial z}$.

-   The magnetic moment $\mathbf{\mu}$ is proportional to the total
    angular momentum $\hat{\mathbf{J}}$ (orbital + spin). For Silver
    atoms in the ground state, $\hat{\mathbf{L}}=0$, so $\mathbf{\mu}$
    is proportional to the electron spin $\hat{\mathbf{S}}$.
    Specifically,
    $\mathbf{\mu}_S = g_e \frac{-e}{2m_e} \hat{\mathbf{S}}$, where
    $g_e \approx 2$ is the electron g-factor. Thus, $\mu_z \propto S_z$.

-   The force $F_z$ deflecting the atoms is therefore proportional to
    the eigenvalue of $\hat{S}_z$.

**Classical Expectation:**

-   If angular momentum (and thus $\mu_z$) could take any continuous
    value corresponding to arbitrary orientations of the magnetic moment
    vector, the atoms would be deflected by varying amounts, resulting
    in a continuous smear on the detection screen.

**Observed Result:**

-   The beam splits into a discrete number of distinct spots. For Silver
    atoms ($s=1/2$), exactly **two** spots are observed, corresponding
    to deflections upwards and downwards.

**Conclusion:**

-   The experiment demonstrates the **spatial quantization** of angular
    momentum projection. The z-component of the angular momentum (in
    this case, spin) can only take on a discrete set of values.

-   For spin-1/2 particles, there are only two possible values for
    $S_z$: $+\hbar/2$ and $-\hbar/2$, leading to the two observed beams.
    This was a radical departure from classical physics and a key
    confirmation of quantum theory.

::: center
:::
::::

::: remark
Spin and Magnetic Moment Connectionrem:spin_mag_moment The lecture's
reference to the atom behaving like a small magnet (\"calamita\")
directly relates to the magnetic dipole moment $\mathbf{\mu}$ associated
with the electron's spin. It is the interaction of this intrinsic

the force and subsequent deflection observed in the Stern-Gerlach
experiment. The quantization of the deflection angles is direct proof of

# Physical Systems for Qubit Implementation {#sec:physical_systems}

The abstract concept of a qubit, a two-level quantum system, finds
concrete realization in various physical platforms. These systems
leverage the inherent quantum properties discussed previously, such as
quantized energy levels ([2](#sec:qho){reference-type="ref+Label"
reference="sec:qho"}) and spin angular momentum
([3](#sec:angular_momentum){reference-type="ref+Label"
reference="sec:angular_momentum"}), to encode and manipulate quantum
information. We will explore some prominent examples, starting with
electron spin and photon polarization, and discuss the crucial
abstraction involved in isolating the qubit dynamics from the full
system description.

## Electron Spin Qubits {#subsec:electron_spin}

As established in [3.3](#subsec:spin){reference-type="ref+Label"
reference="subsec:spin"}, the intrinsic spin angular momentum of an
electron provides a natural two-level system. Being a spin-1/2 particle,
its spin projection along any chosen axis (conventionally the z-axis) is
quantized to two possible values, $m_s = \pm 1/2$.

::: definition
Electron Spin Qubit Basisdefn:spin_qubit_basis The computational basis
states $|0\rangle$ and $|1\rangle$ are typically identified with the
spin-down and spin-up states, respectively:
$$\begin{aligned}        |0\rangle &\equiv |\downarrow\rangle \equiv |s=1/2, m_s=-1/2\rangle \\        |1\rangle &\equiv |\uparrow\rangle \equiv |s=1/2, m_s=+1/2\rangle    \end{aligned}$$
A general state is a superposition
$|\psi\rangle = \alpha |0\rangle + \beta |1\rangle$.
:::

### Interaction and Manipulation

The electron's spin is associated with an intrinsic magnetic dipole
moment $\mathbf{\mu}_S = g_e \frac{-e}{2m_e} \hat{\mathbf{S}}$, where
$g_e \approx 2$ is the electron g-factor, $-e$ is the electron charge,
and $m_e$ is its mass. This magnetic moment allows the spin state to be
controlled using external magnetic fields.

-   **Zeeman Splitting:** In a static magnetic field
    $\mathbf{B}_0 = B_0 \hat{z}$, the interaction Hamiltonian is
    $\hat{H}_{Zeeman} = -\mathbf{\mu}_S \cdot \mathbf{B}_0 \approx \frac{g_e e \hbar}{2m_e} B_0 \sigma_z = \hbar\frac{\omega_L}{2} \sigma_z$,
    where $\omega_L = \frac{g_e e B_0}{2m_e}$ is the Larmor frequency.
    This Hamiltonian lifts the degeneracy between the spin states,
    creating an energy gap
    $\Delta E = E_{\uparrow} - E_{\downarrow} = \hbar\omega_L$. The
    $|0\rangle$ and $|1\rangle$ states become energy eigenstates with
    energies $\mp \hbar\omega_L / 2$ (relative to the midpoint).

-   **Manipulation (ESR):** Applying an additional, time-dependent
    magnetic field $\mathbf{B}_{AC}(t)$ perpendicular to $\mathbf{B}_0$
    (e.g., oscillating at or near the Larmor frequency $\omega_L$)
    induces transitions between $|0\rangle$ and $|1\rangle$. This
    technique, known as Electron Spin Resonance (ESR), allows for the
    implementation of arbitrary single-qubit rotations (gates) by
    controlling the frequency, amplitude, phase, and duration of the
    applied microwave pulses.

### Physical Confinement

To function as a qubit, the electron must be localized and individually
addressable. Common methods include:

-   **Quantum Dots:** Semiconductor nanostructures that confine
    electrons in all three dimensions, acting like \"artificial atoms\"
    with tunable energy levels.

-   **Donor Atoms in Silicon:** Electrons bound to impurity atoms (like
    Phosphorus) embedded in a silicon crystal lattice.

-   **Nitrogen-Vacancy (NV) Centers in Diamond:** Point defects in the
    diamond lattice consisting of a nitrogen atom adjacent to a vacant
    site. The electronic spin state of the NV center exhibits long
    coherence times even at room temperature.

Each platform has advantages and disadvantages regarding scalability,
coherence time (how long the quantum state survives before degrading due
to environmental interactions), and ease of control and measurement.

## Photon Polarization Qubits {#subsec:photon_polarization}

Photons, the quanta of the electromagnetic field, are massless bosons
with spin $s=1$. However, due to their massless nature, the projection
of their spin along their direction of propagation can only take values
$m_s = \pm 1$, corresponding to left and right circular polarizations.
The transverse nature of electromagnetic waves gives rise to two
independent polarization states, which can be used to encode a qubit.

### Polarization Bases

Several convenient orthonormal bases can be used to describe photon
polarization:

-   **Linear (Rectilinear) Basis:** Horizontal ($|H\rangle$) and
    Vertical ($|V\rangle$) polarization.
    $$|0\rangle \equiv |H\rangle, \quad |1\rangle \equiv |V\rangle.$$

-   **Circular Basis:** Right ($|R\rangle$) and Left ($|L\rangle$)
    circular polarization. These relate to the linear basis as:
    $$\begin{aligned}            |R\rangle &= \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(|H\rangle + i|V\rangle) \label{eq:R_pol} \\            |L\rangle &= \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(|H\rangle - i|V\rangle) \label{eq:L_pol}        \end{aligned}$$
    One might choose $|0'\rangle \equiv |R\rangle$ and
    $|1'\rangle \equiv |L\rangle$.

-   **Diagonal Basis:** Diagonal ($|D\rangle$) and Anti-diagonal
    ($|A\rangle$) polarization, rotated by 45 degrees relative to H/V.
    $$\begin{aligned}            |D\rangle &= \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(|H\rangle + |V\rangle) \label{eq:D_pol} \\            |A\rangle &= \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(|H\rangle - |V\rangle) \label{eq:A_pol}        \end{aligned}$$

A general polarization stateA general polarization state is a
superposition $|\psi\rangle = \alpha |H\rangle + \beta |V\rangle$.

### Manipulation and Measurement

Photon polarization can be easily manipulated and measured using
standard optical components:

-   **Polarizers:** Project the photon state onto a specific
    polarization axis, used for state preparation and measurement. For
    example, a horizontal polarizer transmits $|H\rangle$ photons and
    blocks $|V\rangle$ photons. Measuring in the diagonal basis requires
    a polarizer oriented at 45 degrees.

-   **Wave Plates (Retarders):** Birefringent materials that introduce a
    phase shift between two orthogonal polarization components.

    -   *Half-Wave Plate ($\lambda/2$):* Introduces a $\pi$ phase shift.
        Rotates linear polarization (e.g., converts $|H\rangle$ to
        $|V\rangle$ if oriented correctly). Implements transformations
        like the Pauli-X and Pauli-Z gates (depending on orientation and
        basis).

    -   *Quarter-Wave Plate ($\lambda/4$):* Introduces a $\pi/2$ phase
        shift. Converts linear to circular polarization and vice versa
        (e.g., converts $|H\rangle$ to $|R\rangle$ if oriented
        correctly). Implements transformations related to the Hadamard
        gate.

    By combining wave plates and polarizers, arbitrary single-qubit
    unitary operations and measurements in different bases can be
    performed.

::: remark
Pros and Cons of Photonic Qubitsrem:photonic_pros_cons Photons are
excellent carriers of quantum information over long distances due to
their speed and weak interaction with the environment (leading to long
coherence times during flight). However, this weak interaction also
makes it challenging to implement deterministic two-qubit gates, which
are necessary for universal quantum computation. Measurement-based
schemes and non-linear optical effects are often employed to overcome
this.
:::

## Other Qubit Modalities {#subsec:other_systems}

Besides electron spin and photon polarization, numerous other physical
systems are explored for qubit implementation, often leveraging energy
quantization in different contexts:

-   **Trapped Ions:** Individual ions confined by electromagnetic
    fields. Qubits are encoded in stable electronic energy levels
    (optical qubits) or hyperfine ground states (hyperfine qubits).
    Interactions are mediated by shared motional modes (phonons),
    enabling high-fidelity gates.

-   **Superconducting Circuits:** Microfabricated circuits containing
    Josephson junctions, which behave as non-linear inductors. These
    circuits exhibit quantized energy levels when cooled to cryogenic
    temperatures. By designing the circuit parameters, one can create
    anharmonic oscillators (like the \"Transmon\" qubit mentioned in
    [\[rem:qho_qubit_reqs\]](#rem:qho_qubit_reqs){reference-type="ref+Label"
    reference="rem:qho_qubit_reqs"}) where the lowest two levels
    ($|0\rangle, |1\rangle$) can be selectively addressed using
    microwave pulses, effectively isolating a qubit subspace.

-   **Neutral Atoms:** Atoms trapped in optical lattices or tweezers.
    Qubits can be encoded in hyperfine states or Rydberg states (highly
    excited electronic states).

-   **Topological Qubits:** Based on exotic quasiparticles (e.g.,
    Majorana fermions) in condensed matter systems, whose non-local
    properties could offer inherent protection against local
    perturbations, potentially leading to fault-tolerant quantum
    computation.

These systems often involve more complex physics and engineering than
the simple descriptions of spin or polarization might suggest, but they
offer diverse pathways towards scalable quantum computing.

## General Considerations: Separating Qubit State from Full System Description {#subsec:general_considerations}

A critical concept, emphasized in the lecture, is that the simple qubit
state $|\psi_{qubit}\rangle = \alpha|0\rangle + \beta|1\rangle$ is an
abstraction representing only a specific degree of freedom of a much
more complex physical system.

### Spatial Degrees of Freedom (Wavefunction) {#subsubsec:spatial_dof}

Any physical particle used as a qubit carrier (electron, atom, photon)
also possesses spatial degrees of freedom described by a wavefunction
$\psi(\mathbf{r}, t)$. The complete state of the system resides in a
larger Hilbert space, often described by a tensor product:
$$|\Psi_{total}(t)\rangle = |\psi_{spatial}(t)\rangle \otimes |\psi_{qubit}(t)\rangle.    \label{eq:tensor_product_state}$$
For instance, an electron qubit might have a state like
$\Psi(\mathbf{r}, t) = \psi(\mathbf{r}, t) \otimes (\alpha(t)|\downarrow\rangle + \beta(t)|\uparrow\rangle)$.
Here, $\psi(\mathbf{r}, t)$ describes the probability amplitude of
finding the electron at position $\mathbf{r}$ at time $t$, while the
second part describes its spin state.

::: remark
Entanglement between Subsystemsrem:subsystem_entanglement In general,
the spatial and internal (qubit) degrees of freedom can become
entangled, meaning the total state cannot be written as a simple product
state like
[\[eq:tensor_product_state\]](#eq:tensor_product_state){reference-type="ref+label"
reference="eq:tensor_product_state"}. For example, in the Stern-Gerlach
experiment
([\[box:stern_gerlach\]](#box:stern_gerlach){reference-type="ref+Label"
reference="box:stern_gerlach"}), the interaction entangles the spin
state with the spatial trajectory:
$\alpha |\psi_{spatial, \uparrow}\rangle \otimes |\uparrow\rangle + \beta |\psi_{spatial, \downarrow}\rangle \otimes |\downarrow\rangle$,
where $|\psi_{spatial, \uparrow}\rangle$ and
$|\psi_{spatial, \downarrow}\rangle$ represent wavepackets moving along
different paths.
:::

### Temporal Evolution and Effective Hamiltonians {#subsubsec:temporal_evolution}

The time evolution of the total state $|\Psi_{total}(t)\rangle$ is
governed by the full Hamiltonian $\hat{H}_{total}$ via the Schrödinger
equation:
$i\hbar\frac{d}{dt}|\Psi_{total}(t)\rangle = \hat{H}_{total} |\Psi_{total}(t)\rangle$.
This Hamiltonian typically includes terms for the spatial dynamics
(kinetic and potential energy) and the qubit dynamics (e.g., Zeeman
term), as well as any interaction terms:
$$\hat{H}_{total} = (\hat{H}_{spatial} \otimes \hat{I}_{qubit}) + (\hat{I}_{spatial} \otimes \hat{H}_{qubit}) + \hat{H}_{interaction}.$$
Qubit operations (gates) are implemented by applying control fields,
which correspond to adding time-dependent terms to $\hat{H}_{total}$,
ideally designed to act primarily on the qubit subspace (i.e., terms
like $\hat{I}_{spatial} \otimes \hat{H}_{control}(t)$).

The free evolution under $\hat{H}_{qubit}$ (e.g., Zeeman splitting)
leads to phase accumulation. If $|0\rangle$ and $|1\rangle$ are energy
eigenstates with energies $E_0$ and $E_1$, a state
$\alpha|0\rangle + \beta|1\rangle$ evolves as:
$$|\psi(t)\rangle = \alpha e^{-i E_0 t / \hbar} |0\rangle + \beta e^{-i E_1 t / \hbar} |1\rangle = e^{-i E_0 t / \hbar} \left( \alpha |0\rangle + \beta e^{-i (E_1 - E_0) t / \hbar} |1\rangle \right).    \label{eq:qubit_time_evolution}$$
The overall phase $e^{-i E_0 t / \hbar}$ is usually unobservable. The
physically relevant dynamics are captured by the relative phase
$e^{-i \Delta E t / \hbar}$, where $\Delta E = E_1 - E_0$.

::: tcolorbox
To simplify analysis and focus on the controllable dynamics relative to
the fast free evolution, physicists often employ an **effective
Hamiltonian** or transform into a **rotating frame**.

-   By mathematically transforming the state vector (e.g.,
    $|\psi' (t)\rangle = e^{i E_0 t / \hbar} |\psi(t)\rangle$), the fast
    oscillation associated with $E_0$ can be removed.

-   In this rotating frame, the effective Hamiltonian describes the
    evolution relative to the free precession, making the effect of
    control fields (which are often applied at or near the transition
    frequency $\Delta E / \hbar$) clearer.

-   The unitary operators $\hat{U}$ representing quantum gates in
    quantum computing algorithms typically correspond to the evolution
    operators generated by these effective Hamiltonians in the rotating
    frame, acting solely on the two-dimensional qubit subspace
    $\text{span}\{|0\rangle, |1\rangle\}$.
:::

::: remark
Physical Transmission of Qubitsrem:physical_transmission As stressed in
the lecture, when a qubit is physically sent from one location to
another (e.g., Alice sending a photon qubit to Bob), it is the *entire
physical system* (the photon) that travels. This system carries both its
spatial properties (encoded in its wavefunction, determining its path
and timing) and its internal qubit state (encoded in its polarization).
While our logical description focuses on the
$\alpha|0\rangle + \beta|1\rangle$ part, the physical reality involves
the propagation of the complete entity
$|\psi_{spatial}\rangle \otimes |\psi_{qubit}\rangle$. Understanding
this distinction is crucial when considering physical implementation,
communication protocols, and sources of error.
:::

# Review of Qubit Principles {#sec:review_qubit}

Having explored specific physical systems, let's consolidate the
fundamental mathematical and conceptual framework used to describe
qubits, independent of their particular physical realization. These
principles form the core postulates of quantum mechanics as applied to
two-level systems.

## State Representation and Superposition {#subsec:review_state}

::: definition
Qubit State Spacedefn:qubit_state_space A qubit is a quantum system
whose state is described by a vector in a two-dimensional complex
Hilbert space, denoted $\mathcal{H}$. This space is isomorphic to
$\mathbb{C}^2$.
:::

We typically work with a preferred orthonormal basis for this space,
called the **computational basis**, denoted by
$\{|0\rangle, |1\rangle\}$. These basis vectors correspond to the two
distinguishable classical states or measurement outcomes (e.g., spin
down/up, horizontal/vertical polarization, ground/excited energy state).
In matrix representation:
$$|0\rangle \equiv \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}, \quad |1\rangle \equiv \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}.$$
The defining characteristic of quantum mechanics is the principle of
**superposition**. A general state of a qubit, $|\psi\rangle$, can be
any linear combination of the basis states:
$$|\psi\rangle = \alpha |0\rangle + \beta |1\rangle = \begin{pmatrix} \alpha \\ \beta \end{pmatrix},    \label{eq:qubit_superposition}$$
where $\alpha, \beta \in \mathbb{C}$ are complex numbers called
**probability amplitudes**. The state $|\psi\rangle$ represents a
coherent superposition, meaning the qubit exists simultaneously in
relation to both basis states, weighted by the amplitudes $\alpha$ and
$\beta$.

::: proposition
Normalization Conditionprop:normalization This proposition states that
the total probability of finding the qubit in any state must be 1, which
translates to a condition on the state vector's length (norm).

The state vector $|\psi\rangle$ must be normalized, meaning its norm
squared is equal to 1:
$$\left\||\psi\rangle\right\|^2 = \langle\psi|\psi\rangle = (\langle 0|\alpha^* + \langle 1|\beta^*) (\alpha |0\rangle + \beta |1\rangle) = \alpha^*\alpha + \beta^*\beta = \left|\alpha\right|^2 + \left|\beta\right|^2 = 1.        \label{eq:normalization}$$
This condition ensures that the probabilities of all possible
measurement outcomes sum to unity, as required by probability theory.
:::

:::: tcolorbox
While $|\psi\rangle$ lives in $\mathbb{C}^2$ (four real parameters), the
normalization condition
([\[eq:normalization\]](#eq:normalization){reference-type="ref+label"
reference="eq:normalization"}) and the fact that the overall phase of a
quantum state is unobservable reduce the number of independent real
parameters needed to specify a pure single-qubit state to two. We can
write the state (up to a global phase) as:
$$|\psi\rangle = \cos\left(\frac{\theta}{2}\right) |0\rangle + e^{i\phi} \sin\left(\frac{\theta}{2}\right) |1\rangle,        \label{eq:bloch_sphere_param}$$
where $\theta \in [0, \pi]$ and $\phi \in [0, 2\pi)$ are real
parameters. These angles define a point on the surface of a unit sphere
in $\mathbb{R}^3$, known as the **Bloch sphere**.

-   The north pole ($\theta=0$) corresponds to $|0\rangle$.

-   The south pole ($\theta=\pi$) corresponds to $|1\rangle$.

-   Points on the equator ($\theta=\pi/2$) represent equal
    superpositions of $|0\rangle$ and $|1\rangle$, such as
    $|+\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(|0\rangle+|1\rangle)$ (at $\phi=0$)
    and $|i\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(|0\rangle+i|1\rangle)$ (at
    $\phi=\pi/2$).

-   Orthogonal states correspond to antipodal points on the sphere.

The Bloch sphere provides a useful visualization tool for single-qubit
states and the effect of single-qubit gates (which correspond to
rotations of the state vector on the sphere).

::::

## Measurement Postulate and Probabilities {#subsec:review_measurement}

Quantum measurement is fundamentally probabilistic and invasive, meaning
it generally disturbs the state being measured. The standard measurement
postulate describes projective measurements in a chosen basis.

::: proposition
Born Rule for Qubitsprop:born_rule This proposition, known as the Born
rule, provides the fundamental link between the mathematical description
of a quantum state (the amplitudes) and the probabilities of observing
specific outcomes upon measurement. It also describes the state collapse
after measurement.

Consider a qubit in the state
$|\psi\rangle = \alpha |0\rangle + \beta |1\rangle$. If a measurement is
performed in the computational basis $\{|0\rangle, |1\rangle\}$, the
following rules apply:

-   The measurement outcome will be either 0 or 1.

-   The probability of obtaining outcome 0 is
    $P(0) = \left|\alpha\right|^2$. If outcome 0 is obtained, the state
    of the qubit immediately after the measurement collapses to
    $|0\rangle$.

-   The probability of obtaining outcome 1 is
    $P(1) = \left|\beta\right|^2$. If outcome 1 is obtained, the state
    of the qubit immediately after the measurement collapses to
    $|1\rangle$.

The probabilities sum to one,
$P(0) + P(1) = \left|\alpha\right|^2 + \left|\beta\right|^2 = 1$,
consistent with the normalization condition
([\[eq:normalization\]](#eq:normalization){reference-type="ref+label"
reference="eq:normalization"}).
:::

::: remark
Measurement in Different Basesrem:measurement_bases Measurements can be
performed in any orthonormal basis. For example, measuring in the
diagonal basis $\{|+\rangle, |-\rangle\}$ (where
$|\pm\rangle = (|0\rangle \pm |1\rangle)/\sqrt{2}$) corresponds
physically to measuring the Pauli-X operator $\sigma_x$. To find the
probabilities, one first expresses the state $|\psi\rangle$ in the
measurement basis:
$|\psi\rangle = \alpha' |+\rangle + \beta' |-\rangle$. The probabilities
are then $P(+) = \left|\alpha'\right|^2$ and
$P(-) = \left|\beta'\right|^2$. Physically, this might involve rotating
the basis before measurement (e.g., using a Hadamard gate or rotating a
polarizer).
:::

::: remark
Connection to Physical Systemsrem:measurement_physical

-   **Stern-Gerlach:** Measuring the spin projection $S_z$ corresponds
    to a measurement in the $\{|\downarrow\rangle, |\uparrow\rangle\}$
    basis. The probabilities of deflection up or down are given by the
    squared magnitudes of the amplitudes for $|\uparrow\rangle$ and
    $|\downarrow\rangle$ in the initial state.

-   **Photon Polarization:** Passing a photon through a vertical
    polarizer performs a measurement in the $\{|H\rangle, |V\rangle\}$
    basis. The probability of transmission (outcome V) is
    $\left|\beta\right|^2$ if the state is
    $\alpha|H\rangle + \beta|V\rangle$, and the transmitted photon is in
    the state $|V\rangle$.
:::

## Evolution via Unitary Operators {#subsec:review_evolution}

The third fundamental postulate governs how quantum states evolve in
time when the system is not being measured.

::: proposition
Unitary Evolutionprop:unitary_evolution This proposition states that the
time evolution of a closed quantum system (one not interacting with its
environment or being measured) is described by a unitary transformation,
which preserves the quantum state's normalization and is reversible.

The evolution of a closed quantum system is described by a unitary
transformation. If the state of the system at time $t_1$ is
$|\psi(t_1)\rangle$, its state at a later time $t_2$ is given by:
$$|\psi(t_2)\rangle = \hat{U}(t_2, t_1) |\psi(t_1)\rangle,        \label{eq:unitary_evolution}$$
where $\hat{U}(t_2, t_1)$ is a unitary operator mapping the Hilbert
space onto itself. A linear operator $\hat{U}$ is unitary if its adjoint
(conjugate transpose) $\hat{U}^\dagger$ is also its inverse:
$$\hat{U}^\dagger \hat{U} = \hat{U} \hat{U}^\dagger = \hat{I},        \label{eq:unitarity_condition}$$
where $\hat{I}$ is the identity operator.
:::

::: remark
Properties of Unitary Evolutionrem:unitary_props

-   **Preservation of Norm:** Unitary operators preserve the inner
    product between states, and consequently, the norm of state vectors.
    If $|\phi\rangle = \hat{U}|\psi\rangle$, then
    $\langle\phi|\phi\rangle = \langle\psi|\hat{U}^\dagger\hat{U}|\psi\rangle = \langle\psi|\psi\rangle$.
    This ensures that a normalized state remains normalized throughout
    its evolution, consistent with the conservation of probability.

-   **Reversibility:** Since $\hat{U}$ has an inverse
    ($\hat{U}^\dagger$), quantum evolution is reversible. Knowing the
    state at time $t_2$, one can determine the state at $t_1$ by
    applying $\hat{U}^\dagger(t_2, t_1) = \hat{U}(t_1, t_2)$.

-   **Connection to Schrödinger Equation:** For a system described by a
    time-independent Hamiltonian $\hat{H}$, the evolution operator is
    given by:
    $$\hat{U}(t_2, t_1) = e^{-i\hat{H}(t_2-t_1)/\hbar}.\label{eq:evolution_operator_hamiltonian}$$
    The operator $\hat{H}$ is Hermitian ($\hat{H}^\dagger = \hat{H}$),
    which ensures that $\hat{U}$ defined this way is unitary. The state
    evolution $|\psi(t)\rangle = e^{-i\hat{H}t/\hbar}|\psi(0)\rangle$ is
    the solution to the time-dependent Schrödinger equation
    $i\hbar\frac{d}{dt}|\psi(t)\rangle = \hat{H} |\psi(t)\rangle$.
:::

::: remark
Quantum Gates as Unitary Operatorsrem:gates_unitary In quantum
computation, logical operations on qubits are represented by specific
unitary operators called **quantum gates**. Examples include the Pauli
gates ($\hat{X}, \hat{Y}, \hat{Z}$), the Hadamard gate ($\hat{H}$),
phase gates ($\hat{S}, \hat{T}$), and multi-qubit gates like CNOT. These
gates correspond to physically applying carefully controlled
interactions (e.g., timed laser or microwave pulses, as discussed in
[\[subsec:electron_spin,subsec:photon_polarization\]](#subsec:electron_spin,subsec:photon_polarization){reference-type="ref+Label"
reference="subsec:electron_spin,subsec:photon_polarization"}) that
implement the desired Hamiltonian evolution over a specific duration,
resulting in the target unitary transformation on the qubit state(s).
:::

::: remark
Implementing Unitary Operatorsrem:implementing_unitary The lecture
mentioned that \"it's very easy to build\" unitary operators. This
likely refers to the theoretical principle that any unitary
transformation can, in principle, be generated by some Hamiltonian
evolution according to
[\[eq:evolution_operator_hamiltonian\]](#eq:evolution_operator_hamiltonian){reference-type="ref+label"
reference="eq:evolution_operator_hamiltonian"}. Finding the specific
physical interactions (i.e., the Hamiltonian $\hat{H}$) and controlling
them precisely over time to implement a desired $\hat{U}$ with high
fidelity is the central challenge of experimental quantum computing.
While the existence of such Hamiltonians is guaranteed, their practical
realization requires sophisticated experimental techniques and careful
engineering to isolate the system and apply the correct control fields.
:::

This lecture has navigated key concepts from quantum mechanics that form

quantization of spin projection, particularly for spin-1/2 particles
like electrons ($m_s = \pm 1/2$), provides a natural and widely used
two-level system for qubit implementation.

The **Stern-Gerlach experiment**
([\[box:stern_gerlach\]](#box:stern_gerlach){reference-type="ref+Label"
reference="box:stern_gerlach"}) was presented as pivotal historical
evidence confirming the quantization of angular momentum projection
(spatial quantization) and providing strong support for the existence of
electron spin. Its outcome---discrete deflection rather than a
continuous smear---starkly contrasts classical expectations.

We then surveyed specific **Physical Systems for Qubit Implementation**
([4](#sec:physical_systems){reference-type="ref+Label"
reference="sec:physical_systems"}), focusing on **electron spin** qubits
(manipulated via magnetic fields in confined environments like quantum
dots or NV centers) and **photon polarization** qubits (manipulated
using optical elements like wave plates and polarizers). Other
modalities like trapped ions and superconducting circuits were also
briefly mentioned, highlighting the diversity of platforms being
developed.

A crucial point emphasized was the necessary **abstraction** involved in
the qubit model
([4.4](#subsec:general_considerations){reference-type="ref+Label"
reference="subsec:general_considerations"}). The simple state
$\alpha|0\rangle + \beta|1\rangle$ represents only a specific degree of
freedom within a more complex physical entity that includes spatial
wavefunctions and overall temporal dynamics
($|\Psi_{total}\rangle = |\psi_{spatial}\rangle \otimes |\psi_{qubit}\rangle$).
Understanding this separation, often managed via effective Hamiltonians
and rotating frames
([\[box:rotating_frame\]](#box:rotating_frame){reference-type="ref+Label"
reference="box:rotating_frame"}), is vital for appreciating both the
power and the challenges of quantum control.

Finally, we reviewed the core **Qubit Principles**
([5](#sec:review_qubit){reference-type="ref+Label"
reference="sec:review_qubit"}): state representation in a Hilbert space
([\[defn:qubit_state_space\]](#defn:qubit_state_space){reference-type="ref+Label"
reference="defn:qubit_state_space"}), the principle of **superposition**
allowing simultaneous existence in multiple basis states, the

([\[prop:born_rule\]](#prop:born_rule){reference-type="ref+Label"

linked to the Schrödinger equation.

## Key Takeaways {#key-takeaways .unnumbered}

-   **Quantization is Key:** The discreteness of physical properties
    (energy levels in confined systems, angular momentum projections) is
    a fundamental quantum effect that provides the distinct states
    ($|0\rangle, |1\rangle$) needed to define a qubit.

-   **Intrinsic Properties Offer Resources:** Nature provides intrinsic
    quantum properties like electron spin, which inherently form
    two-level systems well-suited for qubit encoding.

-   **Abstraction is Powerful but Incomplete:** The abstract qubit
    formalism ($\mathbb{C}^2$ Hilbert space, unitary gates) is essential
    for developing algorithms and understanding information processing,
    but realizing these operations requires careful management of the
    complex underlying physics of the host system.
